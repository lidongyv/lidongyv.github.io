Keep the Future, Drop the Rollout: Rift for World Action Models
World action models (WAMs) condition robot actions on predicted futures, but iterative video rollout increases deployment latency. We ask whether action generation requires the evolving rollout trajectory or only its future representation. Across four WAMs on 40 simulated robotic manipulation tasks, paired closed-loop interventions show that blocking access to the future cache or reassigning its values changes execution and reduces success. Yet in the evaluated co-denoising settings, reusing one fixed final-clean key/value (K/V) cache throughout action denoising nearly preserves unmodified execution, with 1.71.7–1.91.9 cm end-effector average displacement error. Obtaining this cache still requires iterative video generation. We therefore propose Rift (Rollout-free Imagination via Future Tokens), which uses learned anticipation tokens to construct a complete future K/V cache in one backbone pass. On LIBERO, Rift achieves 98.8%98.8\% overall success, outperforming all evaluated rollout-based methods while yielding a 3.1\bm{3.1}–9.2×\bm{9.2\times} inference speedup. Without further training, it achieves 81.1%81.1\% overall success on the out-of-distribution LIBERO-Plus benchmark, a +9.7+9.7 percentage-point improvement over the strongest evaluated baseline. On RoboTwin, it achieves 92.9%92.9\% and 92.6%92.6\% success on clean and randomized scenes, respectively, the highest among the evaluated methods. On real-world manipulation tasks, Rift achieves 45.3%45.3\% average success, a +6.0+6.0 percentage-point improvement over Fast-WAM-Joint. These results support rollout-free future conditioning without iterative video generation at deployment.

1 Introduction
Figure 1: Success rate vs. deployment latency. Points show mean success rates, and error bars indicate ±1\pm 1 standard deviation across three evaluation runs per method. Latency is measured in milliseconds per action chunk on one A800. Grey squares denote current-only methods; blue circles denote methods that read a future representation produced by rollout. Rift achieves the highest success rate with latency close to the minimum.
World action models couple future video prediction with robot control. A single network predicts a short video and conditions its next actions on that prediction (Yuan et al. 2026; Li et al. 2026; Bi et al. 2025). In matched settings, rollout-based policies achieve higher success than current-only variants. However, iterative video generation makes rollout-based systems incur 3.3×3.3\times to 9.6×9.6\times the latency of current-only deployment (fig. 1).
Fast-WAM drops the future branch after future-prediction co-training (Yuan et al. 2026), whereas PFD distills a future-conditioned correction into the current-only path (Fang, Chen, and Cai 2026). Both reduce latency but retain a gap to policies that explicitly read a generated future. Yet the action expert consumes a future representation, whereas iterative rollout is the process that constructs it. Existing comparisons change both the availability of this representation and the process used to construct it, so their separate contributions remain unresolved. We therefore ask whether a WAM can preserve explicit test-time future conditioning without iterative video rollout.
To separate these factors, we examine the future cache, which contains the keys and values of future tokens at each layer. Joint co-denoising updates this cache at every denoising step, whereas the generate-then-act inverse dynamics model (IDM) uses a fixed final-clean cache computed from the fully denoised future video. Their similar success suggests that the representation, rather than its iterative trajectory, may provide the shared benefit. Success alone cannot reveal what the action reads.
We therefore intervene on this cache. The attention mask prevents video tokens from attending to action tokens, so the cache is an action-independent intervention site. We record it and replay action denoising with non-target inputs fixed, masking attention to future tokens, editing future values under the recorded keys, or replacing both keys and values with one fixed final-clean cache. Each closed-loop intervention uses the same initial state and policy seed as the unmodified policy. We measure success rate (SR) and end-effector average displacement error (EE-ADE), the average drift from the unmodified end-effector trajectory.
Across 2,0002{,}000 paired trials on all 40 LIBERO tasks, masking yields 18.718.7 cm EE-ADE and reduces success from 98.4%98.4\% to 9.7%9.7\%. Spatial permutation and temporal swapping yield similar EE-ADE (14.314.3 and 15.615.6 cm) but sharply different success (65.2%65.2\% and 0.7%0.7\%), showing that the action reads future content at its assigned positions. In contrast, reusing one fixed final-clean K/V cache yields only 1.91.9 cm EE-ADE and 97.9%97.9\% success. Actions depend on future content and its organization, yet one fixed cache nearly preserves execution.
This cache still requires iterative video generation. We therefore ask whether one forward pass can produce an effective, complete future cache. We propose Rift (Rollout-free Imagination via Future Tokens), which places learned anticipation tokens at future temporal positions and fills their per-layer K/V cache with one video-backbone pass. We shape the anticipation states with conditional flow matching, using a distributional objective rather than direct L2 regression to the single observed future. Deployment requires one cache prefill followed by the ordinary action flow, without video diffusion or video decoding at test time.
On LIBERO, Rift achieves 98.8%98.8\% overall success with a 3.13.1–9.2×9.2\times inference speedup over the evaluated rollout-based baselines. Without further training, it achieves 81.1%81.1\% overall success on LIBERO-Plus, a +9.7+9.7 percentage-point improvement over the strongest evaluated baseline. On RoboTwin 2.0, it achieves the highest observed success rates among the evaluated methods in both clean and randomized scenes. On three real-world tasks, Rift achieves 45.3%45.3\% average success, a +6.0+6.0 percentage-point improvement over Fast-WAM-Joint, with 60.7%60.7\% lower action-chunk latency.

2 Related Work
Vision-language-action policies.
Vision-language-action (VLA) policies produce robot actions from visual observations and language instructions (Zitkovich et al. 2023; Kim et al. 2024; Black et al. 2024a; Physical Intelligence et al. 2025; NVIDIA et al. 2025; Shukor et al. 2025; Gemini Robotics Team et al. 2025). Many use diffusion or flow matching to refine a sequence of actions over several steps (Chi et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2024; Wen et al. 2025). These policies provide a useful efficiency reference because they do not also generate future video. Our aim is to retain the benefits of future prediction at similarly low deployment latency.
World action models.
World models predict how an environment changes to support planning and control (Schrittwieser et al. 2020; Hafner et al. 2023; Hansen, Su, and Wang 2024). In robot manipulation, generated future images or videos can guide the choice of actions (Du et al. 2023; Black et al. 2024b; Bharadhwaj et al. 2024; Zhou et al. 2024). Other methods learn video prediction and action generation together (Cheang et al. 2024; Li et al. 2025; Zhu et al. 2025; Cen et al. 2025). Within this family, one design generates a future video before predicting actions, while another refines video and actions together (Ye et al. 2026; Yuan et al. 2026; Li et al. 2026; Bi et al. 2025; Kim et al. 2026). In the former, action generation can use a fixed future representation; in the latter, this representation changes during generation. Both designs incur the cost of iterative video generation. We test whether the action generator needs these successive updates or can reuse one fixed future representation.
Learning from future observations.
Several methods learn from future observations during training while avoiding video generation at deployment. Fast-WAM trains video and action prediction together, but generates actions from the current observation at inference (Yuan et al. 2026). PFD uses a teacher with access to true future observations to train a correction for a policy that uses only current observations (Fang, Chen, and Cai 2026; Vapnik and Vashist 2009; Chen et al. 2019). Other methods retain learned future representations at deployment. FLARE and DreamVLA train internal features to capture future observations or scene properties (Zheng et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2025). Being-H0.7 trains such features with a separate branch that sees future observations and is removed at inference (Luo et al. 2026). EvoScene-VLA uses future observations to supervise a scene representation that is updated by actions and carried across control steps (Zhang et al. 2026). Rift also retains a future representation, but constructs it directly in the form used by the WAM action generator: the key/value cache. One backbone pass fills this cache at every layer, and the action generator reuses it throughout action generation.
Interventions for understanding model behavior.
Probing methods train simple predictors to recover information from a model’s internal features (Alain and Bengio 2016; Belinkov 2022). This shows what information is available, but does not establish that the model uses it. Attention weights alone are also insufficient evidence of use (Jain and Wallace 2019; Serrano and Smith 2019). Causal tracing tests the effect of selected internal representations by changing them and measuring the model’s response (Meng et al. 2022). We apply this idea to future caches and measure changes in executed robot trajectories and task success.
Runtime failure monitoring.
Rao et al. (2026) examine how an action prediction changes during generation to estimate uncertainty and detect potential failures. Our optional monitor compares two estimates of the future and uses their disagreement as a warning signal. This monitor is separate from the action policy and excluded from reported latency measurements; appendix E provides the details.

3.1 The channel we edit
With paired closed-loop interventions, we test whether actions depend on the future cache, whether future values must stay at assigned positions, and whether the complete K/V cache must evolve during denoising. Each model–intervention estimate uses 2,0002{,}000 paired trials across all 4040 LIBERO tasks. Figure 2 reports executed EE-ADE and success rate.
Fast-WAM-Joint, Fast-WAM-IDM, Cosmos Policy, and LingBot-VA construct futures differently but all provide per-layer keys and values associated with future tokens. We call these the future cache; Oracle denotes the unmodified checkpoint used as the reference for interventions.
Because video tokens don’t attend to action tokens, the cache is action-independent given observation (oo), language (ll), and video-generation randomness. This property supports record and replay. The record pass generates video normally and stores per-layer future K/V at every action-denoising step. Value corruptions retain the recorded key trajectory and edit only the matched future values. The final-clean control instead takes the final clean future from Oracle’s iterative generation, prefills its complete K/V once, and reuses that fixed cache at every action-denoising step. All non-target inputs remain fixed. Exact replay of the unedited trajectory reproduces Oracle. Shuffle and noise alter only future values, with recorded keys fixed. We interpret these out-of-distribution perturbations alongside the frozen-present and final-clean K/V controls.

Action chunks are not directly comparable across architectures, so we execute paired policies. For episode ii, intervention II and Oracle share the initial state and policy seed. We exclude the reset pose and use recorded simulator positions rather than integrating predicted actions. When runs end at different times, only their common prefix contributes. We first average EE-ADE over episodes within each task, then average the task means with equal weight. Appendix B defines the bootstrap procedure that resamples tasks. EE-ADE measures drift from Oracle; success measures task completion. High EE-ADE with a similar success rate indicates altered end-effector motion at comparable task performance. When the success rate also decreases, the motion deviation is accompanied by reduced task success.
3.3 Intervention set
The interventions map directly to the three questions. Masking blocks attention to future tokens; norm-matched noise replaces future values; and frozen-present supplies plausible, non-predictive values. Spatial shuffle permutes values within frames, temporal swap exchanges value frames, and final-clean replay replaces the evolving complete cache with the same final-clean K/V at every action step. We apply each supported edit to Fast-WAM-Joint, Fast-WAM-IDM, Cosmos Policy, and LingBot-VA, comparing each with its Oracle reference. Claims are therefore within-model; cross-model magnitudes remain descriptive because architectures and checkpoints differ.
3.4 Finding 1: WAM action experts use future values at their assigned positions
Across all four WAMs, masking yields 11.811.8–20.420.4 cm EE-ADE and reduces success from 98.498.4–98.6%98.6\% to 0.00.0–32.0%32.0\%. Under recorded keys, noise yields 10.510.5–17.917.9 cm and at most 40.9%40.9\% success, while frozen-present values yield 18.118.1–21.321.3 cm and at most 6.5%6.5\%. These within-model effects show that action experts use meaningful future values.
Position also matters: spatial shuffle yields 5.05.0–19.819.8 cm and 0.00.0–84.5%84.5\% success across all four, while temporal swap yields 15.615.6–16.316.3 cm and 0.00.0–69.0%69.0\% on Joint, IDM, and LingBot-VA. Every supported edit changes execution and lowers own-model success, so future values are not an unordered pool. Severity differs by interface: temporal swap is worse for Joint and IDM, spatial shuffle for LingBot-VA, and Cosmos-2 exposes no temporal swap. Thus, position sensitivity is shared, not a universal severity ordering.
3.5 Finding 2: One fixed final-clean K/V cache nearly preserves execution
Finding 1 establishes content and position sensitivity, not whether the complete cache must evolve. Where supported, final-clean replay replaces the entire future-cache trajectory with one final-clean cache, holding both K and V fixed at every action-denoising step.
For Joint and Cosmos-2, final-clean K/V replay gives 1.9/1.71.9/1.7 cm EE-ADE and 97.9/98.2%97.9/98.2\% success; the corresponding Oracle policies reach 98.4/98.4%98.4/98.4\%. These are the smallest nonzero EE-ADEs among compatible edits. IDM and LingBot-VA already expose one fixed final-clean K/V cache, so this replay is identical by construction and structurally N/A.
The fixed cache nearly preserves Oracle execution in Joint and Cosmos-2, but still requires iterative video generation. The full-cache intervention also does not isolate the separate contributions of keys and values. Section 4 tests whether a single forward pass can construct the complete future K/V cache used by the action expert.

4.3 Training
Training uses two forwards through the same video expert per optimization step. Deployment retains only the second forward’s cache prefill.
Native video supervision.
The first forward applies native video-flow loss ℒvid\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{vid}} (Yuan et al. 2026) to a clean-first-frame clip with noised future latents and no anticipation tokens or action supervision. This preserves dynamics supervision while changing only the deployment interface.
Deployment-matched action training.
The second forward matches deployment through input [f0;E][f_{0};E] and its attention mask. We train the action expert on this cache using unperturbed first-frame inputs and Fast-WAM’s action flow-matching loss ℒact\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{act}} (Yuan et al. 2026). After 70%70\% of the configured curriculum horizon, the probability and scale of first-frame latent noise rise linearly from zero to 0.30.3 and 0.060.06 times the latent standard deviation. Examples with perturbed first-frame inputs retain ℒFM\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}} and ℒprobe\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{probe}} but are excluded from the action loss. 5 Experiments
5.1 Setup
Implementation.
For LIBERO, Fast-WAM, Fast-WAM-Joint, Fast-WAM-IDM, and Rift share the same Wan2.2-5B (Team Wan et al. 2025) Fast-WAM backbone, training data, and 2020k-step budget, so differences come from the future interface rather than scale or data. We additionally compare with the Fast-WAM-based released PFD checkpoint (Fang, Chen, and Cai 2026) and the embodied-pretrained LingBot-VA (Li et al. 2026). Unless stated otherwise, Rift uses conditional flow matching, while Rift-L2 uses direct L2 regression. Latency is milliseconds per action chunk on one A800 under each method’s own denoising configuration; Rift’s figure covers its cache prefill and action denoising and excludes optional diagnostic readouts. In the tables, Fut. attn. denotes attention to future tokens; rollout denotes iterative future generation at deployment.
Benchmarks.
LIBERO (Liu et al. 2023) has 4040 tasks across its Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long suites. We evaluate each method in three runs of 2,0002{,}000 trials (5050 episodes per task) and report the mean and standard deviation across runs. For out-of-distribution (OOD) evaluation on LIBERO-Plus (Fei et al. 2026), we run Fast-WAM, Fast-WAM-Joint, Fast-WAM-IDM, and Rift with one rollout on each of the benchmark’s 10,03010{,}030 variants, without further training. RoboTwin 2.0 (Chen et al. 2025) has 5050 bimanual tasks under clean and domain-randomized scenes. Following Yuan et al. (2026), the matched Fast-WAM/Joint/PFD/Rift family trains on 2,5002{,}500 clean-scene and 25,00025{,}000 randomized demonstrations for 3030k steps, with externally pretrained LingBot-VA for comparison. Each checkpoint is evaluated for 100100 trials per task in each setting.

6 Conclusion
Paired closed-loop interventions show that the evaluated WAMs depend on the content and organization of their future caches. In Joint and Cosmos-2, one fixed final-clean cache nearly preserves execution, although obtaining it still requires iterative video generation. Rift constructs a future K/V cache in one backbone pass and reuses it throughout action denoising. Across the evaluated simulation benchmarks and three real-world tasks, it achieves higher observed overall success than the evaluated baselines. On LIBERO and the real robot, its inference latency remains close to current-only Fast-WAM. These results support retaining future conditioning without iterative video generation at deployment.

Appendix A Training implementation details
Loss normalization.
For ℒvid\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{vid}}, we average unreduced squared error over channels and space, then valid latent frames; for ℒFM\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}, over each patch vector, then valid future patches. Both receive the native video-flow timestep weight per sample before the batch mean. For ℒact\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{act}}, we average over action dimensions, mask padding, average over the horizon, and apply the base action-timestep weight before the batch mean. The coefficients of ℒvid\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{vid}} and ℒact\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{act}} are 11. λFM\lambda_{\mathrm{FM}} and λprobe\lambda_{\mathrm{probe}} stay at 11 for the first 70%70\% of the curriculum, then cosine-decay to 0.20.2; the stopped-gradient probe cannot affect the deployed representation.
Deployment-matched perturbation.
After 70%70\% of the curriculum, perturbation probability and standard deviation rise linearly from 00 to 0.30.3 and from 00 to 0.060.06 times the latent standard deviation, respectively. Training examples with perturbed first-frame inputs retain ℒFM\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}} and ℒprobe\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{probe}} but are excluded from ℒact\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{act}}.
Diagnostic probe.
An RMS-normalized linear probe gives Y^L2=gω​(RMS⁡(stopgrad⁡(Sϕ)))\hat{Y}_{\mathrm{L2}}=g_{\omega}(\operatorname{RMS}(\operatorname{stopgrad}(S_{\phi}))) with ℒprobe=mean⁡((Y^L2−Y)2)\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{probe}}=\operatorname{mean}((\hat{Y}_{\mathrm{L2}}-Y)^{2}) over all batch prediction elements; gradients reach only gωg_{\omega}.
Gradient routes.
ℒvid\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{vid}} updates the video expert and output head, but not the action expert; ℒact\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{act}} updates the action expert and, through attended video states, the video expert and anticipation tokens; ℒFM\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}} updates the video expert, anticipation tokens, and FM head; detached ℒprobe\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{probe}} updates only the linear probe.
Architecture and training configuration.
All in-house models share Wan2.2-5B’s pretrained video DiT, text encoder, and video VAE (Team Wan et al. 2025). The action expert reuses the video branch at da=1024d_{a}=1024 (11B action; 66B total), with horizon H=32H=32. Multi-camera images are concatenated before the VAE; video is temporally downsampled 4×4\times to 99 frames per chunk. Both branches use Fast-WAM’s continuous flow matching. We sample u∼𝒰[0,1)u\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1), set σ=5​u/(1+4​u)\sigma=5u/(1+4u) and t=1000​σt=1000\sigma, and reuse the video scheduler for the FM head. Deployment uses 1010 action flow-matching steps, classifier-free guidance 1.01.0, and no video denoising or VAE decoding. Training uses AdamW at 10−410^{-4} learning rate, 0.010.01 weight decay, cosine annealing, mixed precision, and gradient clipping at 1.01.0, for 2020k LIBERO or 3030k RoboTwin 2.0 steps. Relative to Fast-WAM (Yuan et al. 2026), Rift preserves these settings and adds only anticipation tokens, ℒFM\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}, and the diagnostic probe.

Appendix E Optional L2–FM uncertainty warning
The final model retains direct L2’s deterministic future-latent readout as a stopped-gradient diagnostic probe. Both heads read the same anticipation states without feeding the controller; this controller-independent comparison runs only as an optional monitor and is excluded from policy-only deployment and reported latency. Let μL2=Y^L2∈ℝm×dy\mu_{\mathrm{L2}}=\hat{Y}_{\mathrm{L2}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d_{y}} denote the probe estimate, let xiFM∈ℝm×dyx_{i}^{\mathrm{FM}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d_{y}} be the iith of KK samples from the conditional-FM head, and let x¯FM=K−1​∑ixiFM\bar{x}_{\mathrm{FM}}=K^{-1}\sum_{i}x_{i}^{\mathrm{FM}} denote their sample mean at the current replanning step. With D=m​dyD=md_{y} latent elements, we compute the discrepancy using mean squared differences over those elements:
dratio=D−1​∥μL2−x¯FM∥F2(K​D)−1​∑i=1K∥xiFM−x¯FM∥F2+ϵ,d_{\mathrm{ratio}}=\frac{D^{-1}\lVert\mu_{\mathrm{L2}}-\bar{x}_{\mathrm{FM}}\rVert_{F}^{2}}{(KD)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\lVert x_{i}^{\mathrm{FM}}-\bar{x}_{\mathrm{FM}}\rVert_{F}^{2}+\epsilon},
(7)
where ϵ=10−12\epsilon=10^{-12} stabilizes the denominator. Large dratiod_{\mathrm{ratio}} means that the probe point estimate departs from the FM mean beyond the dispersion of the FM samples. It is a warning statistic, not a failure probability: both heads may still agree on the same wrong future. Unlike the action-flow acceleration of Rao et al. (2026), this score compares two estimators in future-latent space.
FM sampling and monitoring frequency.
At each replanning step, we generate K=4K=4 samples as one batch, starting from standard Gaussian noise with fixed seeds 0,1,2,30,1,2,3. Each sample uses five explicit Euler steps on a time grid with shift parameter 5.05.0. The update is xj+1=xj+Δ​σj​vψ​(xj,τj,Sϕ)x_{j+1}=x_{j}+\Delta\sigma_{j}\,v_{\psi}(x_{j},\tau_{j};S_{\phi}), where SϕS_{\phi} denotes the final anticipation states and τj\tau_{j} is the FM time at integration step jj. After an initial wait of 3030 environment steps, the policy replans every 1010 environment steps and executes 1010 actions from each predicted chunk. We compute one monitoring score per replan. Let n≥1n\geq 1 index these observations and dn=dratio​(n)d_{n}=d_{\mathrm{ratio}}(n); observation nn is recorded at environment step tn=30+10​(n−1)t_{n}=30+10(n-1). The CUSUM updates once per observation:
Sn=max⁡(0,Sn−1+dn−μd−0.25​σd),S_{n}=\max\!\left(0,S_{n-1}+d_{n}-\mu_{d}-0.25\sigma_{d}\right),
(8)
with S0=0S_{0}=0. An alarm occurs at the first nn for which Sn>ηS_{n}>\eta and is assigned environment step tnt_{n}.
Calibration.
Of the 2,0002{,}000 evaluation episodes (4040 LIBERO tasks with 5050 episodes each), we use the 1,9671{,}967 successful episodes for calibration and exclude all 3333 failures. Pooling the 29,05929{,}059 monitoring scores from successful episodes gives the arithmetic mean μd=0.54082563\mu_{d}=0.54082563 and population standard deviation σd=0.11140236\sigma_{d}=0.11140236, computed with divisor 29,05929{,}059. Thus, the reference level in eq. 8 is μd+0.25​σd=0.56867622\mu_{d}+0.25\sigma_{d}=0.56867622.
Following the episode-peak CUSUM calibration rule of Rao et al. (2026), we compute one peak Pi=maxn⁡Sn(i)P_{i}=\max_{n}S_{n}^{(i)} for each successful calibration episode. With M=1,967M=1{,}967 and the preselected level α=0.001\alpha=0.001, the conformal order statistic is
r=⌈(M+1)​(1−α)⌉=1,967,η=P(r),r=\left\lceil(M+1)(1-\alpha)\right\rceil=1{,}967,\qquad\eta=P_{(r)},
