Abstract
An autonomous vehicle must foresee the consequences of its actions, especially in the rare, time-critical situations
where crashes happen. A world model offers this foresight, but only within the coverage of its training data, which
poses an exploration–exploitation dilemma. Within this coverage a planner can hardly improve on the data, and
beyond it the model’s errors mislead it. Logs of competent driving rarely contain the failures that would widen the
coverage, and agents that learn from interaction gain them only by failing themselves, without being shown what to do
instead. We proposeSAFER , a framework that learns offline from failures that others make, judged by an expert, and
keeps learning online from its own driving. Offline, partially trained agents and scripted, recoverable perturbations
Stage failures in simulation, and an expertAnnotates every visited state with its action and value, so that much of what
the planner explores is covered before it drives. In planning, a chunked latent world modelForesees the consequences
fast enough for real-time use, and a risk-aware sampling-based plannerEvaluates candidate maneuvers by their return
minus a bounded collision penalty. Online, a loop continuouslyR efines the model wherever this driving leaves the
coverage of the offline data. In closed-loop evaluation against the compared methods, SAFER reaches the highest
route completion and reward per step on the CARLA Leaderboard routes,0.95and0.77from a privileged bird’s-eye
view, and the highest episode reward in MetaDrive,53.4in regular and49.0in safety-critical traffic. Project page:

1. Introduction
Autonomous vehicles must handle rare, time-critical interactions in which a late or wrong decision cannot be
undone, the situations in which crashes happen. Learning-based driving systems have made rapid progress in closed-
loop driving, yet these situations remain the main obstacle to deploying them, because the data they learn from contain
few such situations and fewer of their consequences (Li et al., 2026).
Handling such a situation requires anticipating what an action will lead to before taking it. An experienced driver
does this before every maneuver, imagining how the next seconds will unfold and choosing accordingly. A policy
trained by imitation or by model-free reinforcement learning does not. It maps the current observation directly to an
action. A world model, in contrast, predicts how the scene will evolve under a candidate sequence of actions, so that a
planner can compare the imagined futures before committing to one (Ha and Schmidhuber, 2018; Hafner et al., 2020;
Hansen et al., 2024; Levy et al., 2026).
A world model, however, can only foresee what its training data contain; we call the states and actions that these
data represent itscoverage. This poses an exploration–exploitation dilemma for any planner that relies on a world
model (Fig. 1b). The dilemma is the choice between exploiting known options, whose outcomes are predictable,
and exploring new ones, which may pay off in the long run but whose outcomes are uncertain (Berger-Tal et al.,
2014; Hills et al., 2015; Wilson et al., 2021). A planner that exploits what the model knows, choosing among the
actions that the data cover, can rely on the predictions but can hardly do better than the driving in the data. To do
better, it can explore actions that the data seldom contain, such as braking later or pulling out earlier than competent
drivers do, and the states that its own decisions lead to. There the predictions are unreliable, and because the planner
selects the candidate with the highest predicted value, it is drawn to exactly the candidates whose value the model
overestimates (Chua et al., 2018; Janner et al., 2019; Yu et al., 2020). Experience of these actions is what corrects the
model. Gathering such experience is the role of exploration in reinforcement learning (Curi et al., 2020; Sekar et al.,
2020), but in driving the experience beyond competent driving is the experience of failure, which cannot be gathered
on public roads.

Existing approaches differ in where this experience comes from (Fig. 1a,b). Logs and demonstrations contain
little of it. World models trained on logs of human or expert driving (Hu et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024a,b; Zheng
et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2022) see what competent drivers do and rarely what follows from a mistake of the ego vehicle,
and asked what would have followed an action other than the logged one, they can miss what would actually have
happened (Zhang et al., 2026); behavior cloning on such data does not explore at all, and an expert who never makes
mistakes cannot show how to recover (Ross et al., 2011; Codevilla et al., 2019). Methods that learn from interaction
gather the experience by failing: reinforcement-learning agents and world models learned online fail in simulation,
typically over millions of steps (Zhang et al., 2021; Toromanoff et al., 2020; Li et al., 2024), RaWMPC exposes
its world model to hazardous behavior through a risk-aware interaction strategy (Sun et al., 2026), and closed-loop
training methods generate the scenarios in which the current policy is likely to fail (Zhang et al., 2023; Sheng et al.,
2026a; Li et al., 2026). These failures are the learner’s own. The learner has to cause them, over many trials that
would be unacceptable on public roads, and each failure teaches it only the outcome, a collision or a low reward, not
what should have been done instead.
We let the world model learn instead from failures that others make, judged by an expert. We call the resulting
framework SAFER, after its five stages, which Fig. 1c groups into three parts. Offline (top), safety-critical failures
are staged deliberately in simulation and annotated by an expert. Partially trained agents and scripted, recoverable
perturbations drive until their failures run to their consequences, so that the world model learns what mistakes lead to,
while the expert records at every visited state what should have been done instead. In planning (middle), a chunked
world model foresees the consequences of candidate maneuvers, and a risk-aware planner evaluates them by their
predicted return minus a price on collision risk. Online (bottom), the world model is refined on the planner’s own
driving. SAFER thus broadens the coverage of the world model offline, so that much of what the planner explores is
already covered, and extends it online, gradually, wherever the planner moves beyond it (Fig. 1c).
SAFER is inspired by how people resolve the same dilemma as they grow up (Fig. 2, top). A child is not left to
discover the consequences of every choice alone. Parents praise what works and point to negative examples, so that
the child learns what leads where without having to fail first. The beliefs formed in this way, which choices pay off
now, which goals are worth pursuing and which behavior is dangerous, let the child weigh a choice before making it.
Later, school and work keep reshaping these beliefs in situations the parents never showed, while the core ones stay.
Such learning does need failures, but failures that others make, where they can be afforded, judged by someone who
knows what should have been done. SAFER follows the same course (Fig. 2, bottom). Like parenting, its offline data,
staged and annotated by the expert, provide a wealth of experience to grow up with. Like the child, the world model
foresees what leads where and forms the same beliefs, as predictions of reward, value and collision risk, by which the
planner evaluates each choice. Like school and work, the online stage keeps the planner learning from its own driving.

Driving already uses failures of this kind to widen the data of imitation learning. Logged trajectories are perturbed
and the learner is trained to recover from them (Bansal et al., 2019), noise is injected into an expert’s driving while
the expert keeps labeling (Laskey et al., 2017), and the interventions of safety drivers are recorded.
Behavior cloning on the expert’s labels learns to recover rather than to repeat the mistakes that produced the data,
and the resulting prior is a head of a chunked latent world model (Section 3.3) of the kind introduced by SimDist (Levy
et al., 2026), which imagines a whole chunk of the future in one pass rather than step by step and so keeps planning
fast. A collision-risk head (Section 3.4) learns from the recorded collisions of this corpus to predict the probability that
a candidate action sequence leads to a collision, and it is trained so that braking harder does not raise the predicted risk.
The risk-aware model predictive path integral (MPPI) planner (Williams et al., 2017) (Section 3.5) scores candidate
action chunks by their predicted return minus a bounded collision penalty. The online loop (Section 3.6) adapts the
dynamics, the reward scorers and the risk head on the planner’s own driving and keeps the prior and the value fixed,
while the planner scores with slowly moving copies of the adapted heads. Observations and evaluation follow the
privileged bird’s-eye-view (BEV) setting of Roach (Zhang et al., 2021) in CARLA (Dosovitskiy et al., 2017) and the
closed-loop benchmark of CAT (Zhang et al., 2023) in MetaDrive (Li et al., 2023).
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• A formulation of the exploration–exploitation dilemma of world-model-based planning in driving, in which
searching beyond the coverage of the world model can improve on the data, but there the errors of the model
bias the choice of action, and the experience that would correct them consists of failures. The bias and the regret
it causes are stated formally in Section 3.5.
• A data recipe in which the world model learns from failures that others make, judged by an expert. Partially
trained agents and scripted, recoverable perturbations drive in simulation until their failures run to their conse-
quences, and the expert annotates every visited state with its action and value, so that the data cover much of
what the planner explores.
• A fast chunked latent world model with a collision-risk head that is trained to be monotonic in braking and
foresees collisions in recorded driving before they happen, without false alarms, and a risk-aware MPPI planner
that scores candidate maneuvers by their predicted return minus a bounded collision penalty and plans in real
time on a single GPU; the imagination of the world model carries the information on which driving decisions
depend, such as a traffic light that is about to change.
• An online adaptation rule that extends the coverage from the planner’s own driving by fine-tuning the reward
scorers and the risk head together with the dynamics, while the planner scores against slowly moving copies of
the adapted heads; adapting these heads, and not only the dynamics, makes every type of collision rarer.

• A closed-loop evaluation that isolates the contribution of each stage in CARLA, including towns without train-
ing data, and compares SAFER with the baselines, against which it reaches the highest route completion and
reward per step in CARLA and the highest episode reward in MetaDrive, in regular and safety-critical traffic;
one turn of a data flywheel, which retrains the system on data targeted at the scenarios that the offline corpus
covers least, makes it safer still.

2. Related work
2.1. Learning-based driving policies
Learning-based driving policies are trained mainly by imitation learning and by reinforcement learning. Imitation
learning maps observations to the actions of an expert, from conditional imitation of human driving (Codevilla et al.,
2018) to privileged experts that see the simulator state and teach sensorimotor students (Chen et al., 2020; Zhang et al.,
2021). A policy trained on the expert’s states compounds its own errors once it leaves them, and demonstrations that
contain no mistakes contain no corrections either (Ross et al., 2011; Codevilla et al., 2019); imitation is also prone to
causal confusion, in which the policy keys on correlates of the expert’s action rather than on its causes (de Haan et al.,
2019). DAgger lets the student drive and has the expert label the states it visits, which is how Chen et al. (2020) and
Zhang et al. (2021) train their students, and DART injects noise into the expert’s actions (Laskey et al., 2017). Both
widen the states around the expert, but neither shows what follows from driving badly.
Reinforcement learning learns from outcomes instead. Model-free agents reach strong closed-loop performance
in CARLA (Toromanoff et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2021), and the PPO agent of Zhang et al. (2021) is the expert we use
in CARLA, but such agents are shaped by millions of simulator steps under a hand-designed reward. Vision-language
models have recently been used to enrich this reward and to guide training (Huang et al., 2025; Qu et al., 2026; Huang
et al., 2026). Chen et al. (2021) gain sample efficiency by assuming that the world does not react to the ego vehicle,
which removes exactly the interactions that matter for safety. Safety-critical scenarios are generated by perturbing
other road users (Ding et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2021) or interactively with a multimodal large language model (Sheng
et al., 2025), and closed-loop training methods train the policy on such scenarios (Zhang et al., 2023; Niu et al., 2024;
Sheng et al., 2026a; Li et al., 2026). All of these learn from the failures of the policy that is being trained.
The corpus of SAFER (Section 3.2) takes DAgger one step further. The states are visited by drivers that are
deliberately worse than the expert, partially trained checkpoints of an RL agent, and by perturbations of the ego’s own
actions that run until a consequence occurs, and the expert annotates each state with its action and value. A policy can
learn recoveries from these labels, and a world model can, in addition, learn what the mistakes lead to.
2.2. Model-based reinforcement learning and planning
A world model predicts how the environment evolves under the agent’s actions, so that the agent can learn or plan
in imagination (Ha and Schmidhuber, 2018). Agents use it in two ways. The Dreamer family trains an actor and a critic
on imagined trajectories (Hafner et al., 2020, 2021, 2025a), and its most recent member trains agents entirely inside a
world model of complex environments (Hafner et al., 2025b). PlaNet, TD-MPC and SimDist instead plan online with
the model (Hafner et al., 2019; Hansen et al., 2022, 2024; Levy et al., 2026), searching over action sequences with
sampling-based optimizers such as the cross-entropy method and model predictive path integral control (Williams
et al., 2017; Pinneri et al., 2021). Online planning keeps the objective explicit at decision time, so that it can be
extended, for example with the conditional value-at-risk of the sampled returns (Yin et al., 2023).
Both uses share a weakness. The agent optimizes against the model, and the optimizer is drawn to the errors of
the model in regions the model was not trained on. One line of work keeps the agent where the model is reliable,
through probabilistic ensembles (Chua et al., 2018), short model rollouts branched from real data (Janner et al., 2019)
or uncertainty-penalized rewards (Yu et al., 2020). This is the exploitation side of the dilemma of Section 1, and it
gives up the improvement that lies beyond the coverage of the model. Another line widens the coverage by exploring,
acting optimistically on the epistemic uncertainty of the model (Curi et al., 2020) or planning towards expected
novelty (Sekar et al., 2020), which in driving means failing. SAFER widens the coverage from the data side instead.
The failures that a planner would explore are staged offline and annotated by an expert, and the model is re-fitted
online on the states that the planner actually reaches.
2.3. World models for autonomous driving
Driving world models differ mainly in what they learn from. Models trained on logs of competent driving generate
future scenes (Hu et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024a), select among candidate trajectories by their predicted futures (Wang
et al., 2024b; Zheng et al., 2025), or, like MILE, learn a latent world model and a driving policy jointly from expert
demonstrations (Hu et al., 2022); conditioned on an action other than the logged one, such models can miss what
would actually have happened (Zhang et al., 2026). Other models learn from the agent’s own interaction. Think2Drive
trains a policy inside a world model that it learns online (Li et al., 2024), and InDRiVE and ExploreVLA explore by
4
curiosity, rewarding the disagreement or the uncertainty of the world model’s predictions (Khanzada and Kwon, 2025;
Sheng et al., 2026b). Closest to our aim, RaWMPC folds the predicted probabilities of driving events into the cost of
model predictive control and exposes its world model to hazardous behavior through a risk-aware interaction strategy,
without expert demonstrations (Sun et al., 2026), and CorrRisk-WM predicts the hazard that surrounding agents pose
to the corridor of each candidate trajectory (Guo and Langari, 2026).
SAFER also learns from failures, but from failures that other drivers make offline and that an expert annotates, so
that its data record both what a mistake leads to and what should have been done at the states before it; logs contain
few such failures, and models that learn from their own interaction have to cause them. As in RaWMPC, the predicted
probability of an event enters the planning cost, but the risk head of SAFER is supervised by the staged failures and
trained so that braking does not raise its prediction, and the planner keeps an explicit objective rather than a policy
distilled from the model.
2.4. Continual adaptation of world models
A world model pretrained on one data distribution meets another once its own planner drives, and it has to keep
learning from the planner’s experience without losing what it learned before. SimDist adapts a world model pretrained
in simulation to a real robot by re-fitting only its latent dynamics on the planner’s rollouts (Levy et al., 2026), and
AdaWM first identifies whether a loss in performance on a new driving task stems from the policy or from the
dynamics and then updates the dominant part with low-rank updates (Wang et al., 2025). In driving, the closed-loop
distribution also shifts the quantities that the planner scores, such as speeds, gaps and near-misses. SAFER therefore
adapts the reward scorers and the collision-risk head together with the dynamics, since all three can be supervised
from recorded outcomes, and keeps the prior and the value fixed, since only the expert can teach them. Every update
mixes offline and online windows, so that the corpus is not forgotten, and the planner scores with slowly moving
copies of the adapted heads, the target-network idea of value learning (Mnih et al., 2015), so that its objective does
not change with every update.
3. Methodology
This section describes SAFER. At every control step, the planner imagines the consequences of candidate action
sequences with a learned latent world model, searches for the sequence with the best predicted return, penalized by
the predicted probability that the sequence leads to a collision, and executes its first action. The five stages of SAFER
map onto four parts, the four panels of Fig. 3:
(a)StageandAnnotate: a corpus ofmixed-qualitysimulated experience, driven by a reinforcement-learning (RL)
expert, by weaker checkpoints of an RL agent trained with the expert’s recipe and by scripted perturbations, in
which every step is labeled with the expert’s action and value (Section 3.2);
(b)Foresee: achunked latent world modelthat encodes an observation, predicts the latent states of a whole chunk
of the future under a candidate action sequence in one forward pass, and reads the imagined trajectory with a
physically decomposed reward, a continuation probability, an ensemble of value heads and a behavior-cloned
action prior (Section 3.3), together with acollision-risk headthat predicts the probability that a collision has
occurred by each future step; the risk head is trained only on the simulator’s recorded outcomes and regularized
so that braking harder does not raise the predicted risk (Section 3.4);
(c)Evaluate: arisk-aware sampling-based plannerthat runs model predictive path integral control (MPPI) on the
world model, seeded with the behavior-cloned prior and penalized by the predicted collision risk (Section 3.5);
(d)Refine: anonline adaptationloop that keeps the dynamics, the reward scorers and the risk head consistent with
the planner’s own driving, while the planner scores against slowly moving exponential-moving-average (EMA)
copies of them (Section 3.6).
The design follows the decomposition of Simulation Distillation (SimDist) (Levy et al., 2026): an observation
encoder, a chunked latent dynamics model conditioned on an explicit history, reward and value heads that read the
imagined trajectory, and a base policy that warm-starts a sampling-based planner. The world model of SAFER is
trained on data that other policies generate and is then asked to plan on the states that its own planner visits. A planner
for safety-critical driving must also know what makes a candidate good or dangerous, and its model must keep pace
with the planner’s own driving. SAFER therefore predicts the reward through its physical factors and the value with
an ensemble, prices the predicted collision probability in the planner’s score with an event-based risk head trained to
be monotonic in braking, and adapts the scorers online while the planner scores with their EMA copies.

3.4. Learned collision-risk head
The return in the planner’s score, Eq. (28) below, already penalizes safety events, through the reward and the value
bootstrap. A collision, however, enters the return only through the reward of a single imagined step, diluted by the
discount and, when it ends the episode, by the survival probability, and its effect on the score depends on how well the
reward head extrapolates to a rare event. A dedicated head that answers a simpler question,has a collision happened
by imagined stepk, can be trained as a classifier with class re-weighting, is bounded, and yields a penalty whose scale
is under direct control. Similar event heads are used by RaWMPC (Sun et al., 2026); our head differs in its cumulative
target and in the monotonicity constraint below, and it is supervised only by the simulator’s recorded safety events,
never by any hand-written rule.

3.6. Online adaptation with EMA scoring targets
A world model trained on the corpus of Section 3.2 has learned from the states that other drivers visited, not from
those that its own planner visits. SimDist addresses the analogous simulation-to-reality gap by collecting rollouts with
the planner and re-fitting only the latent dynamics on them. We adopt the same loop (Algorithm 1) with two changes.
First, the reward scorers and the risk head are adapted as well, because the closed-loop distribution of speeds, gaps
and near-misses differs from the corpus and these heads, not only the dynamics, determine which candidate wins.
Second, the planner does not score with the live scorers but with EMA copies that follow the live parameters slowly,
so that the objective it optimizes does not move with every gradient step.

Interleaved collection and updates.The planner drives in the environment to which it is to be adapted, which need
not be the one in which the corpus was collected; in our experiments these are the training environments of each
benchmark, so that its test routes and scenarios stay held out (Section 4). The planner’s transitions are kept in an in-
memory buffer of fixed size that holds only the most recent ones, and the parts of episodes still being driven are added
to it whenever windows are drawn (the sizes are given in Appendix C). After every burst ofn b = 256environment

the most recent burst completed or extended. Online windows use the sameC+Hlayout and terminal padding as
offline ones.

4. Experiments
After the setup in both simulators (Section 4.1), the experiments ask four questions. What do planning with the
world model, the risk term, online adaptation and the data flywheel each contribute in closed loop (Section 4.2)? How
does the final system compare with the baselines, in CARLA (Section 4.3) and on the safety-critical benchmark of
CAT in MetaDrive (Section 4.4)? Which of the states that the ego can reach within the horizon does the world model
expect to be unsafe, and how reliable is this expectation (Section 4.5)? Does the imagination of the world model carry
the information on which driving decisions depend (Section 4.6)? Section 4.7 then reports the cost of training and
planning.
4.1. Experimental setup
SAFER is evaluated in CARLA and in MetaDrive (Fig. 4); this subsection gives the setup in each.
4.1.1. CARLA
Simulator, expert and behavior policies.We use CARLA (version 0.9.15; Dosovitskiy et al., 2017), and the agent
observes only the privileged BEV mask stack and the ego state of Section 3.1 (Fig. 4c). The expert is the publicly
released Roach PPO agent (Zhang et al., 2021) after11.8M environment steps. Its actor provides the action labels
aet , its critic the value labelsv et , and its feature extractor gives the architecture of the world-model encoder and
initializes it. The intermediate drivers are theM= 13checkpoints of a Roach agent that we trained from scratch with
Roach’s code and hyper-parameters, saved between0.2and4M training steps, so that the bank spans a wide range of
competence rather than one level of it.
Offline corpus.The three sets of Section 3.2, summarized in Table 1, are collected in the four training towns of
Roach’s benchmark, Town01, Town03, Town04 and Town06 (Fig. 4a), on unbounded random routes with randomized
traffic density; every checkpoint contributes the same number of transitions toDint. Two scripted events are used; their
durations and the parameters of the action noise of Eq. (2) are listed in Table C.1 in Appendix C. Aholdfires when
the ego has been stopped and the reward’s desired speed jumps up, as when a light turns green or a lead vehicle moves
off, and holds full brake while the expert would already be starting. Alate brakefires when the expert brakes for a
hazard in the ego’s path and holds the previous throttle, but only if the expert could still stop before the hazard after
the override. An episode terminates at the first collision, red-light or stop-sign violation, or when the ego is blocked or
leaves its lane, so every safety event ends its episode in this corpus. The reward subtracts a terminal penalty, its fifth
factor, of1at any termination and1 +v t, with the ego speedv t in m/s, at a safety violation, so that a fast collision
is penalized more than a slow one. The corpus contains the effect that a planner must be able to foresee. Among the
states in which the expert brakes for a hazard ahead, the collision rate within the next1.6s is roughly half as high
when the executed action brakes as when it does not.
Offline training.Both offline stages train on the union of the three sets with uniform sampling, with no re-weighting
between the expert, intermediate and targeted sets. Stage 1 trains the world model, the reward, continuation and value
heads and the action prior for1.0M steps, together with the scene-factor head of Section 4.6 as an auxiliary task,
whose loss is RMS-normalized like the others and added to Eq. (21) with weight0.5. Stage 2 adds the risk head,
drops the scene-factor head and continues all parameters jointly for0.5M steps, with the additional windows before
terminals of Section 3.3. The remaining settings are listed in Table C.1 in Appendix C.
Online adaptation.The online loop (Algorithm 1) drives the four training towns of the corpus under its termination
rules, using the planner of Section 3.5 with the risk penalty (W= 50, pricing collisions only) and scoring against the
EMA copies. It runs for10 6 environment steps,27.8h of driving.

4.1.2. MetaDrive
Benchmark.We use the closed-loop benchmark of CAT (Zhang et al., 2023), which consists of500scenarios of the
Waymo Open Motion Dataset (Ettinger et al., 2021) replayed in MetaDrive (Li et al., 2023),400for training and
100for testing, observed through the101-dimensional vector of Section 3.1 (Fig. 4d–f). Every test scenario is driven
twice, first in its logged traffic (regular) and then with an opponent that CAT’s generator places on the predicted
trajectory most likely to collide with the ego’s regular drive (safety-critical, Fig. 4e); the first vehicle contact ends the
episode. The metrics are those of CurricuVLM (Sheng et al., 2026a): episode reward, road completion, total distance,
crash rate (the share of episodes that end in a vehicle contact) and average speed.
Data and model.The offline corpus applies the recipe of Section 3.2 to the400training scenarios under CAT’s
training rules, in which a vehicle contact costs−1without ending the episode. The expert is a CAT agent, TD3
trained with CAT’s closed-loop adversarial training and CAT’s code, for106 steps, and eight of its checkpoints, saved
between2.5×10 4 and7.5×10 5 steps, are the intermediate drivers, perturbed by the noise process of Section 3.2 with
the parameters of the CARLA corpus (Section 4.1.1). Every scenario is driven in its logged traffic and again against
CAT’s opponent: by the intermediate drivers, by the noise-free expert, which also faces the opponents generated
against the intermediate drivers, and by the expert with targeted hazard brakes, in which the ego brakes for0.5–1s
when a road user ahead on its route closes in and the expert then resumes. CAT’s expert seldom brakes for hazards, so
this event takes the place of the late brake of CARLA as the source of braking near hazards. The corpus holds44,800
episodes and3.2M transitions; the expert labels every state with its action, and the value labels are the noise-free
expert’s values, estimated by policy evaluation on the corpus with CAT’s discount of0.99, which the planner also
uses asγ. The world model is that of Section 3.3, with an observation encoder initialized from the first two layers of
the expert’s actor and CAT’s reward components as reward factors; dynamics, heads, action prior and risk head are
trained together in a single stage of2.5×10 5 steps.
Planner and online adaptation.On this benchmark the risk head predicts three events, vehicle contact, contact with
an object and leaving the road, and the planner weights all three,ω= (1,1,1)in Eq. (26), since leaving the road
also ends an episode;W= 50as in CARLA. Two settings differ from CARLA because of the benchmark. CAT’s
traffic replays the logs and does not react to the ego, so slowing down can cause a rear-end contact, and the braking-
monotonicity term of Eq. (25) is not used during online adaptation. The action prior, distilled from CAT’s TD3 expert,
often acts at the bounds of the action range, where a plan carried over from the previous step lags behind it, so every
MPPI step starts from the prior’s current chunk instead of the shifted previous plan of Section 3.5. SAFER is evaluated
after10 6 steps of online adaptation on the training scenarios. We train three SAFER systems with different seeds,

Five controllers share the stage-2 checkpoint and differ only in how it is used, and a sixth arm retrains it:
•BC prior: the behavior-cloned priorπ θ of Eq. (13) executed directly, no planning;
•MPPI: planning with the reward, continuation and value heads and no risk penalty (W= 0), i.e. SimDist’s
planner on our model;

weights, i.e. SimDist’s adaptation rule on our model;
•+ online adaptation: the same planner on the model adapted online for10 6 steps, the full SAFER system. The
snapshot of the same run after5×10 5 steps is evaluated as well, for the effect of training length;
•+ data flywheel: one turn of a loop that aims new data at the scenarios that an analysis of the offline corpus
finds covered least and retrains the system. These are encounters with pedestrians. Of the21,287recorded
collisions analyzed in Section 4.5, only2,896involve a pedestrian, and the risk head predicts them at the step at
which they happen less often than collisions with vehicles (Fig. 6b). The corpus is therefore extended by6.5M
transitions, collected like the rest of it on random routes in the training towns. Of these,4.0M come from the
intermediate drivers, and2.5M from the expert with noise and late brakes, facing scripted pedestrians that step
into the ego’s path on straight roads and at the exits of left turns (Table 1). Both offline stages are retrained with
the same recipe, and the new model is adapted online for10 6 steps with the full system’s settings.

Replacing the prior by the planner without a risk term does not change the driving score significantly. The planner
completes more of each route and is blocked less often (7.0%of the episodes against14.9%), but it runs more red
lights (5.7against1.3per100km), so its infraction penalty falls, and its acceleration command changes six times
more from one step to the next.
Adding the risk penalty raises the driving score by0.08, and the gain comes from the infraction penalty. Collisions
with pedestrians fall from20.6to12.7per100km, and the share of routes completed without a collision rises from

Besides how long the loop runs, the gain depends on what it adapts. When the loop adapts the dynamics alone, as
SimDist does, and leaves the reward scorers and the risk head at their offline weights, the driving score rises by only
0.02over the offline planner, which is not significant, and stays0.05below the full system’s, a significant difference
that comes from the infraction penalty. The adapted dynamics alone make vehicle collisions and red lights run rarer
(22.7and1.0per100km, against28.3and4.3offline), but pedestrian collisions do not fall (14.7against12.7) and the
planner is blocked more often (8.3%of the episodes against4.8%). The significant gain of online adaptation therefore
requires adapting the reward scorers and the risk head along with the dynamics, as SAFER does (Section 3.6).
One turn of the data flywheel makes the system safer. Its infraction penalty rises by0.05, a significant gain.
Pedestrian collisions fall from8.8to5.5per100km and vehicle collisions from20.6to16.0. The retrained system
is also more cautious. It is blocked in11.0%of the episodes, against6.6%, and completes less of each route (0.950
against0.967), so its driving score rises by0.03, which is not significant.

4.5. A reachability view of the world model’s risk prediction
This subsection examines the world model’s ability to predict risk from the perspective of reachability. Among
the states that the ego can reach within the horizon, which does the world model expect to be unsafe, and how
reliable is that expectation? At every step, the candidate chunks that the planner scores, each rolled out by the world
model, sample the part of the ego’s reachable set that the planner explores within the horizon; we call it the imagined
reachable set. Along every candidate the world model predicts the reward of each imagined step, and the risk head the
probability that a priced safety event has occurred by then. Laid onto the ground, these probabilities mark a risk area,
the places of the reachable set where the risk head expects a collision or another priced event. The planner does not
threshold it. Eq. (26) charges every candidateWtimes the mean of its probabilities over the horizon, so a candidate
loses score in Eq. (28) the longer, and the deeper, it runs through the risk area, while the predicted rewards enter its
return. Read this way, the planner looks for a path through the reachable set that earns a high predicted return and
stays clear of the risk area. The risk area is only as reliable as the risk head; we first test the head offline on recorded
collisions (Fig. 6) and in closed loop on the system’s own collisions, and then show the reachable set and its risk area

and whose episode contains the3s before the impact plus a full context. The corpus contains21,287such events.
Each is assigned to a collision partner from the BEV at the impact step: a pedestrian within4m, otherwise a vehicle

within the segment or the horizon after it. For each event the decision time slides from3.0s to0.1s before the impact

are reported: ˆP1.6, the probability that a collision has occurred by the end of the1.6s horizon, whose correct value

as well as expected. Control segments go through the same procedure, and their ˆP1.6 measures false alarms. The
events and controls come from the training corpus, so this test measures what the head has learned to predict from the

4.6. Explaining the imagination
The planner chooses among imagined futures, so the imagination has to carry the information that driving deci-
sions depend on. We check this with a scene-factor head, a two-layer MLP that reads a latent as the state of the traffic
light on the route (none, red light or stop sign, yellow, green) and the presence of a vehicle ahead, a vehicle directly
ahead, a pedestrian near the path, a stop line on the route and the ego on its route, with targets from rule-based labels
of the BEV frames. It is trained with the world model in stage 1, on the context and the imagined latents (Section 4.1),
and the planner never uses it, so this subsection reads the imagination of the stage-1 world model. For recorded win-
dows of the corpus, the model receives the16recorded steps of history and the16actions the driver executed next,
and the head reads each imagined latent.
Table 6 compares the state read1.6s ahead in the imagination with the label of the recorded frame, and with
copying the state read at the decision time. Over random windows the factors rarely change within the horizon, and
the imagination is as accurate as copying. It does not invent changes. Where the state does change, copying is almost
always wrong for traffic lights, while the imagined light is right in77%of the windows; for pedestrians and the
vehicle ahead the imagination also does better than copying (57%against51%and49%against30%). Fig. 8 turns
the imagined factors of three recorded situations into a sentence. The model foresees a red light turning green and a
yellow light turning red within0.1s of the recorded change, and pedestrians beside the road coming near the ego’s
path (1.1s imagined,0.9s recorded). The imagination thus carries the state of traffic lights and the movement of other
road users on which the planner’s decisions depend.
4.7. Computational efficiency
SAFER plans with a small model of15.5M parameters in CARLA (15.3M for the world model with its heads,
0.2M for the risk head) and14.3M in MetaDrive. Its training is also inexpensive on at most two Quadro RTX
6000 GPUs. The offline training takes66h in CARLA and7h in MetaDrive, and the online adaptation over10 6
environment steps41h and8h. Table 7 gives the inference time per driving decision in CARLA, from the observation
to the action, on one of these GPUs with a batch of one, and the decision frequency it allows. The behavior-cloned

four times the10Hz at which the agent acts; even the slowest of the2,000decisions (42ms) would allow24Hz.
The risk term and the online adaptation do not change this cost. A small world model is thus enough. With15.5M

5. Conclusion
A world model lets an autonomous vehicle weigh the consequences of its actions before it acts, but it is reliable
only within the coverage of its training data. A planner that exploits the model within this coverage can hardly improve
on the data, a planner that explores beyond it is misled by the errors of the model, and the experience that would widen
the coverage is the experience of failure. SAFER navigates this exploration–exploitation dilemma by learning from
failures that others make, where they can be afforded, judged by an expert. Partially trained agents and scripted,
recoverable perturbations fail in simulation, an expert annotates every visited state, and a chunked latent world model
learns from this corpus to foresee consequences as reward factors, expert values and collision probabilities. A risk-
aware MPPI planner scores candidate maneuvers by their predicted return minus a bounded collision penalty, and an
online loop refines the dynamics, the reward scorers and the risk head on the planner’s own driving while the planner
scores against slowly moving copies of them.
In CARLA, the risk head foresees recorded collisions before they happen without raising a false alarm on collision-
free driving, and in closed loop each stage contributes where it is meant to. On76routes, the risk term raises the
driving score of the planner by0.08, mainly through fewer collisions with pedestrians, and online adaptation by a
further0.07, with fewer collisions of every type, a gain that adapting the dynamics alone does not reach; together
with planning they raise the driving score of the behavior-cloned prior from0.65to0.77, and one turn of the data
flywheel, which adds targeted pedestrian data, makes the system safer at a driving score of0.80, with the highest
route completion and reward per step in the comparison with the baselines. In MetaDrive, SAFER reaches the highest
episode reward and road completion in the comparison with the baselines, in regular and in safety-critical traffic. The
imagination of its world model carries the information on which driving decisions depend, such as a traffic light that
is about to change, and with15.5M parameters the planner decides at43–45Hz on a single GPU.
The recipe needs only a driver that is allowed to fail recoverably and an expert that can be queried at the states
it visits. A proving ground with a safety driver offers both, which makes the transfer of SAFER from privileged
bird’s-eye-view observations in simulation to sensor-based driving its natural next step.
