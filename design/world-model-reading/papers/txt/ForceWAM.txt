ABSTRACT
Expert demonstrations show how to complete contact-rich tasks, but provide lim-
ited coverage of how interaction forces respond to different actions. Task-agnostic
play provides broader experience of contact dynamics without specifying task
goals. To fully leverage these two types of data, we introduce ForceWAM, a
force world action model that decouples knowledge of task-compatible interaction
outcomes from knowledge of how local actions change contact. A world model
learns from successful demonstrations to jointly predict future video and six-axis
force/torque trajectories. Together, these futures describe the expected evolution
of motion and contact during the task. A separate inverse dynamics model
(IDM) learns from both task and play, mapping current observations and recorded
visual-wrench futures to their associated robot actions. Play data thus provide
knowledge of force responses for goal-directed execution. At deployment, the IDM
reactively translates generated futures into actions, reusing them with fresh wrench
feedback between world-model updates. Across real-robot force-regulated sliding,
box flipping with and without disturbances, and ring placement, ForceWAM
achieves 85-95% mean success, surpassing world-modeling and reactive baselines.
Our experiments further show that adding task-agnostic play data improves success
by 15-45 percentage points, demonstrating its value for learning contact-rich
execution. Project page and code are available athttps://force-wam.github.io/.

1 INTRODUCTION
Robotic manipulation is often cast as predicting actions that reach a desired visual state. Yet contact-
rich tasks are decided not only by where the robot moves, but by how interaction forces evolve along
the way. Two visually similar configurations can correspond to insufficient contact, damaging force, or
a jam. Classical force control therefore places interaction feedback at the center of execution (Hogan,
1985), while recent learned systems show that force and touch improve contact-rich manipulation (Liu
et al., 2025; Xue et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026; Zhuo et al., 2026).
We call this learning problemforce-centric manipulation: a policy must not merely sense force, but
learn the physical regularities needed to regulate it. Recent methods learn adaptive compliance or
hybrid force-position control (Hou et al., 2025; Fang et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026c). We aim to learn
the full force-centric manipulation task directly from data. However, the main obstacle is a mismatch
between where these regularities appear in data and how policies learn them.
Expert demonstrations, which we calltask data, show successful task execution. A box-pushing
demonstration at roughly 10 N, for example, need not show how force changes when the robot presses
farther, backs away, or encounters a disturbance. By contrast,task-agnostic play datarecord ex-
ploratory pressing, sliding, and release, providing additional visual-wrench transitions and associated
robot actions. Play transitions need not demonstrate successful behavior for a given task instruction,
but they still provide recorded future-action pairs for inverse-dynamics learning. End-to-end force-
aware learners (Yu et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026) still couple two questions in one task-supervised
mapping:whattransition advances the task, andwhich actionrealizes that transition. Our question
is: can we use these two data sources more effectively to learn the distinct knowledge each provides?
Video planning with inverse dynamics provides a foundation for learning actions from interaction
outcomes: recorded transitions supervise the inverse model, while generated futures guide execution
(Du et al., 2023; Li et al., 2026b). For contact-rich manipulation, a visual future can leave the desired
evolution of contact forces unspecified. Force- and tactile-aware models have demonstrated the value
of predicting contact signals and using them for reactive control (He et al., 2026; Ma et al., 2026). We
study visual-wrench futures as the condition for learning execution from task and play, associating
actions with both the motion and the contact evolution they produce.
We introduceForceWAM(Figure 1), which uses visual-wrench futures to decouple knowledge
of task-compatible interaction outcomes from knowledge of how local actions change contact.
Successful task demonstrations train the force world model (ForceWM) to jointly generate future
video and six-axis wrench trajectories. The inverse dynamics model (IDM) learns from task and
play segments to predict the robot actions that realize a visual-wrench future. During training, it
conditions on the current observation and the future recorded in each segment. Any segment that
records actions and their resulting motion and contact outcomes can supervise this action prediction,
even if it does not complete a task. At deployment, ForceWM-generated futures replace the recorded
future conditions used in IDM training, specifying the interaction that execution should realize. The
IDM reuses each generated reference within an action chunk, reactively updating its predictions with
newly measured wrench feedback.
We evaluate ForceWAM on force-regulated sliding, box flipping with and without disturbances,
and ring placement, achieving 85-95% mean success across the four evaluation conditions. Our
experiments assess the benefit of added play and the role of visual-wrench future conditioning in
execution. Our contributions are:
• We formulate contact execution as inverse dynamics conditioned on visual-wrench futures.
This formulation allows different types of interaction data to supervise action prediction.
• We propose ForceWAM, whose visual-wrench future interface decouples knowledge of
task-compatible interaction outcomes from knowledge of local action-contact responses.
ForceWM learns from successful demonstrations. The IDM learns from task and play,
mapping generated futures to actions with causal wrench feedback between refreshes.
• Across four real-robot evaluation conditions, we show that added play improves success
by 15-45 percentage points with task demonstrations, ForceWM, and IDM optimizer up-
dates held fixed. Future-conditioning ablations further show the benefit of wrench futures
alongside visual futures, with measured wrench feedback retained.

2 RELATEDWORK
Force-aware manipulation.Force and tactile feedback provide contact information that is difficult
to infer from vision alone (Lee et al., 2019; Suresh et al., 2024; Ai et al., 2024). ForceMimic couples
force-motion demonstration capture with hybrid imitation, while ForceVLA integrates force into
vision-language-action policies and ForceVLA2 extends this line to hybrid force-position control
(Liu et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026c). Beyond incorporating force as an observation,
reactive policies use newly measured contact signals to update actions during execution (Calandra
et al., 2018). RDP combines a slow latent policy with a fast tactile executor, whereas ImplicitRDP
realizes within-chunk force feedback through causal attention in a single diffusion network (Xue
et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026). FA-RDP adapts inference frequency to manipulation phase, and
LIFT combines reactive force injection with online corrective supervision (Zhuo et al., 2026; Wang
et al., 2026). ForceWAM decouples knowledge of task-compatible interaction outcomes from
knowledge of how local actions change contact: ForceWM models what a successful interaction
should look and feel like, while an inverse dynamics model (IDM) turns that future into robot actions
and reactively updates them with within-chunk wrench feedback. The IDM is conditioned on the
current observation and visual-wrench future, which lets it learn from both task and play transitions.
World action models.World action models connect future observation prediction with robot action
generation (Bi et al., 2026a;b; Cai et al., 2026), as exemplified by LingBot-V A, Cosmos Policy,
DreamZero, FACT, and GE-Act 2.0 (Li et al., 2026a; Kim et al., 2026; Ye et al., 2026; Peng et al.,
2026; AgiBot Research Team et al., 2026). A related route separates video planning from action
inference (Xu et al., 2025): UniPi translates generated visual plans with a separately trained IDM,
and VERA develops a Jacobian-based IDM that can learn independently from self-play (Du et al.,
2023; Li et al., 2026b). Contact-aware models extend prediction to force and tactile dynamics for
planning and reactive control (Higuera et al., 2026; Zheng et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026; Madan
et al., 2026; NeoteAI Team & Fudan TEAI Team, 2026). Among these, FAWAM jointly predicts
actions and wrenches and trains residual corrections from human interventions and policy rollouts
(He et al., 2026). TacPAC uses live tactile observations and cached tactile-action predictions to
correct an existing action chunk, with its corrector supervised against demonstrated actions (Ma et al.,
2026). ForceW AM separates learning task-compatible outcomes from learning local action-contact
responses while retaining a force-aware reactive inference process. Successful demonstrations train a
visual-wrench future generator, while task and play windows directly train an IDM on their recorded
futures and associated actions. At deployment, the IDM uses the predicted visual-wrench future
as a task-conditioned reference and reactively corrects each action with newly measured wrench
feedback within the cached future window. Thus, the two modules are decoupled in what they learn
but connected through force feedback during execution, rather than using a visual future only as a
static planning target. This makes task-agnostic contact transitions a direct source of supervision for
executing task-conditioned visual-wrench futures.

3.2 PREDICTION FROM TASK DATA
For a trajectory window starting at time t, we construct the future prediction target gt = (zt,W t),
Wt = ( ¯wt+1, . . . ,¯wt+Hg ), where zt encodes future wrist images and Wt is a normalized future
wrench trajectory. Thus, gt is the target visual and contact trajectory for ForceWM. The corresponding
robot actions are modeled separately by the IDM. Appendix B specifies frame offsets and encodings.
ForceWM models pϕ(gt |I t,w t, ℓ)from successful task demonstrations. Its backbone is a pretrained
spatiotemporal video diffusion model. We arrange its latent input as five semantic slots: a blank prefix,
the current wrench, the current image, the future wrench trajectory, and the future visual latent. The
first three slots are clamped throughout denoising, while the last two are jointly generated. A fixed
packing adapter repeats low-dimensional wrench values over the spatial extent of a video-latent slot.
This construction reuses one spatiotemporal backbone for the two future modalities while retaining
separate visual and wrench outputs.

3.4 SLOW-FAST EXECUTION OFFORCEWAM
ForceWM updates slowly, but the IDM can rapidly adjust actions with new wrench feedback. Thus,
we use a temporally causal structure to enable streaming IDM inference. Furthermore, the slow-fast
executor coordinates periodic ForceWM predictions with these reactive action updates.
Temporally causal IDM.The IDM separates conditions by update rate. For a training window, slow
encoders map the current observation and regularized recorded future to fixed tokens, while a recurrent
fast encoder represents the wrench prefix available at action offset k, eCslowt =E slow(It,w t, egt),
Cfastt,k =E fast(wt:t+k). The current image is encoded by a convolutional visual backbone, the current
wrench by a linear adapter, a separate convolutional adapter encodes the future visual latent, and
linear adapters encode the future wrench samples. A unidirectional recurrent encoder produces one
fast token per measured wrench, enabling streaming action prediction as new feedback arrives.
Training processes the full synchronized action and wrench window in parallel, but applies lower-
triangular masks to both action self-attention and action-to-measured-wrench attention. Action token
k accesses only noisy action tokens 0:k and measured wrench tokens 0:k, ensuring that its inputs
are available during streaming inference. A complementary mask hides reference-wrench tokens at
window offsets ≤k , leaving only later reference samples visible to that action token. This reduces the
training-to-inference shift when generated references replace recorded futures. Future visual tokens
remain globally visible. These masks implement the token-wise condition in Equation 3 without
future-feedback leakage.

prediction. In the synchronous inference process, let r denote the sensor request that starts a slow

4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Tasks.We evaluate three real-robot task families under four contact-rich conditions.Slidingdrags
the end effector along the centerline of a force scale while targeting a normal force of 10 N. Success
requires full-path completion and satisfaction of the 5-15 N contact-force criterion.Box flipping
requires sustained contact and a controlled pushing force to carry the box over its edge, and its
disturbed variant (Flip-D) adds an external push during contact.Hanoi ring placementseats a
held ring onto a 10 mm pole, where contact force decides whether the ring seats or jams. All four
conditions are contact-rich and use six-axis wrench feedback. They differ in the kind of contact they
demand—continuous force maintenance, sustained pushing, rejection of an external disturbance, and
fine seating contact.
6
Datasets.We collect 200 task demonstrations and 200 play episodes for each of the three task
families. Flip and Flip-D share the same training data. Both sources provide synchronized wrist
RGB, six-axis TCP wrench measurements, and robot actions. Task demonstrations record successful
task executions. Play is collected without an evaluation-task instruction and consists of exploratory
pressing, sliding, and making and breaking contact. These recorded visual-wrench transitions provide
additional supervision for IDM training.
Baselines.We compare against force-reactive policies RDP (Xue et al., 2025) and ImplicitRDP
(Chen et al., 2026), along with ForceVLA (Yu et al., 2025). We also include world-action models
Cosmos Policy (Kim et al., 2026), Fast-WAM (Yuan et al., 2026), and FA-WAM (He et al., 2026),
as well as π0.5 (Black et al., 2025). In Table 1, unmarked rows use task demonstrations without
action-labeled play, and +Play marks the variants additionally given play. For the +Play variants of
ImplicitRDP and Cosmos Policy, task demonstrations are augmented with play trajectories. Cosmos
Policy uses an empty language condition for play and learns to predict the recorded actions.
Evaluation.Each evaluation run consists of 20 trials per task from matched initial states, under the
same safety limits and controller interface. A trial succeeds if the task completes without a safety stop
or human intervention. For Sliding, success additionally requires full-path completion and satisfying
the5-15N contact-force criterion. We report mean success rates.

in RDP and ImplicitRDP, and force-aware fusion in ForceVLA, expose the contact state but do not
by themselves learn a stronger goal-conditioned action response to force changes. Because these
methods rely on successful demonstrations, they may not observe enough variation in such response
behavior outside nominal contact conditions. ForceW AM combines a task-conditioned wrench future
with an IDM trained on task and play, giving execution a contact reference and broader action-force
experience. The Sliding sequences in Figure 3 illustrate this contact demand: RDP and FA-WAM

pushes, compared with 60% and 40% for FA-WAM, the strongest external comparator in both
conditions. In Figure 3, ForceW AM retains tool-box contact through nominal and disturbed rotation,
while the baseline cases lose contact before completing the flip. An external push can change the
box-tool relationship, so repeating a nominal motion need not preserve the contact needed for rotation.

ForceWM supplies the anticipated box motion and loading, while the IDM updates actions using
measured wrench feedback. This provides a mechanism for retaining the flipping objective while
adapting local execution, consistent with the higher success in both conditions.
Hanoi ring placement yields a different baseline ordering: Fast-WAM reaches 65%, and Cosmos

however, does not determine whether the ring seats under contact. Conditioning the IDM on the
future seating geometry and accompanying wrench evolution gives local force responses a specific
placement outcome to pursue. In the Hanoi comparison (Figure 3), ForceW AM adjusts the ring pose
and seats it on the pole. All three baseline examples fail to complete placement.

5 CONCLUSION
We presented ForceWAM, which decouples knowledge of task-compatible interaction outcomes
from knowledge of how local actions change contact. Task demonstrations train a visual-wrench
future model, while task and play data train an inverse dynamics model that translates generated
futures into actions with force feedback. Real-robot experiments show higher task success than the
evaluated baselines and consistent gains from play. This suggests that using task and play data to learn
complementary knowledge offers a promising path to scalable, stable contact-rich manipulation.

A HARDWARE SETUP AND DATA COLLECTION
All real-robot experiments use a single Flexiv Rizon 4S 7-DoF collaborative arm equipped with joint
torque sensors and a six-axis force/torque (F/T) sensor at the end effector (Figure 6). The end-effector
configuration depends on the task. Hanoi ring placement uses a Robotiq 2F-85 parallel-jaw gripper.
For box flipping (Flip and Flip-D) and Sliding (Slide), a joystick and custom compliant fingertips are

We collect Hanoi data via virtual-reality (VR) teleoperation using a Meta Quest 3. For box flipping
and Sliding, the joint torque sensors support kinesthetic teaching during data collection, while the
end-effector F/T sensor records contact forces and torques. Across tasks, this F/T sensor supplies the
six-axis wrench observations used by ForceWM and the IDM. A wrist-mounted RGB camera records
visual observations. For Hanoi, we also use a third-person camera.

B VISUAL-WRENCH REFERENCE CONSTRUCTION
Recorded training futures and ForceWM predictions use the same representation. From the current
image at time t, the visual target samples future wrist frames at offsets {4,8,12,16} and encodes
them into one 16×56×56 video latent. The wrench target contains the next 16 six-dimensional TCP
wrench measurements. Force and torque components are normalized independently by a min-max
map nF fitted on the ForceWM training split. The same frozen nF is used for recorded future
trajectories and online ForceWM predictions. The measured causal wrench stream supplied to the
IDM uses a separate min-max map nI fitted on its training data. The future-wrench stream remains
in then F coordinates and passes through its IDM input normalizer unchanged.
ForceWM uses the temporal input convention of its pretrained video tokenizer. Its 17-frame pixel
sequence is organized as one blank frame, four copies of a current-wrench placeholder, four copies of
the current wrist image, four future-wrench placeholders, and the four sampled future wrist frames.
The video tokenizer temporally compresses this sequence into five 16×56×56 latent slots: blank,
current wrench, current image, future wrench, and future visual content. The first three are clamped
conditions. Only the last two are noised and predicted.
A fixed adapter flattens the current 6-D wrench or the future 16×6 trajectory and periodically repeats
it across the corresponding video-latent-shaped slot. Decoding averages the complete repeats for
each component. This parameter-free packing lets the wrench and video slots share the pretrained
spatiotemporal backbone while keeping the two generated outputs separate. If a complete future
modality crosses an episode boundary, its generated slot is excluded from the loss rather than
supervising on padded values.
C ROBOT ACTION REPRESENTATION
Each robot action comprises a Cartesian TCP-pose target and a gripper command. In the training
data, it is represented by the TCP pose and gripper state at the next recorded timestep. Poses are
13
expressed relative to the base TCP pose at the start of the prediction window. Rotation is represented
by roll, pitch, and yaw. At deployment, the predicted relative pose is composed with the cached base
TCP pose and converted to the absolute pose-gripper command accepted by the robot controller.
The implementation inherits an auxiliary prediction target from ImplicitRDP (Chen et al., 2026). In
addition to the executable relative pose-gripper action, it predicts a force-derived virtual pose and
a scalar stiffness. This auxiliary block is trained jointly with the executable block, but is discarded
after inference: the robot receives only the decoded TCP pose and gripper width. The auxiliary target
regularizes representations learned from the IDM’s multimodal inputs. After relative-pose conversion,
the full diffusion target has 14 dimensions and an 18-step prediction horizon. Only the prefix aligned
with the current slow window is eligible for execution.
D ARCHITECTURE AND TRAINING DETAILS
ForceWM.We initialize the spatiotemporal backbone from the Cosmos Predict 2B weights
(NVIDIA et al., 2025) and train ForceWM directly on task demonstrations. No intermediate task-
specific pretraining is used. The model takes a wrist-camera image, a six-dimensional TCP wrench,
and a task instruction (e.g., “flip box”). Visual inputs are encoded at448×448. The five-slot latent
is denoised by the shared Cosmos video diffusion backbone with frame-replacement conditioning. A
hybrid EDM noise distribution draws 70% of noise scales from the configured log-normal distribution
and 30% uniformly from [1,85] . The sigma-weighted MSE is evaluated only on the future-wrench
and future-visual slots. The task instruction is supplied through its pretrained T5 text embedding. We
train for 20,000 iterations on eight GPUs with batch size 8 per GPU, hold out the final 30 episodes for
validation, and save a checkpoint every 1,000 iterations. The optimizer uses learning rate3×10 −5.
IDM.The IDM encodes the current 224×224 wrist image with a ResNet-34 whose normalization
layers are GroupNorm. The resulting 7×7 feature map is projected into 49 tokens of width 768,
and the current 6-D wrench is linearly projected into one additional token. The 16×56×56 future
visual latent is encoded by Conv3×3(16,256) -GroupNorm-SiLU, pooled to a 2×2 map, and linearly
projected into four 768-D tokens. Each of the 16 future 6-D wrench samples is independently
projected into one 768-D token. A one-layer, unidirectional GRU with hidden size 512 followed by a
linear projection produces a 768-D token for every measured wrench in the causal prefix.
The action denoiser linearly embeds each 14-D noisy action and adds learned temporal positions.
The diffusion timestep uses a sinusoidal embedding. A learned projection and a two-layer Mish MLP
encode the fixed condition memory. A seven-layer pre-normalized Transformer decoder of width 768,
four attention heads, and 4× feed-forward expansion predicts velocity for all 18 action tokens during
training. Its action self-attention mask and its cross-attention mask over GRU wrench tokens are both
lower triangular. A complementary mask hides every future-wrench reference token whose window
offset is less than or equal to the queried action token. Therefore action token j can use noisy-action
and measured-wrench tokens 0:j, the current image and wrench, reference-wrench tokens at later
window offsets, and all fixed future-visual tokens. Processing the full window in one forward pass is
thus parallel teacher-forced training with the same causal information boundary used by streaming
inference, rather than access to future measured feedback. The action horizon is 18 and the model
observes one current image. Deployment refreshes the slow reference after six actions, so only tokens
0:5 are eligible for execution. In particular, the two tokens beyond the 16-step reference horizon are
supervision-only.
For the play-scaling study, we sample training windows at random from the pooled task and selected
play trajectories. The action model uses a 100-step squared-cosine diffusion schedule and velocity
prediction. Training adds ϵ+ 0.1ϵ ′ to the normalized action while computing the velocity target from
ϵ. Inference uses five deterministic DDIM steps with η= 0 , leading timesteps [80,60,40,20,0] , and
clips the predicted normalized clean action to [−1,1] before denormalization. Image augmentation
consists of a center crop, rotation in [−5◦,5 ◦], and color jitter during training. Stochastic image
augmentation is disabled online.
To reduce the recorded-to-predicted future shift, IDM training applies future-conditioning augmenta-
tion. It first selects the temporal lookup, then drops the entire future condition with a fixed probability,
followed by independent modality dropout, additive noise, and multiplicative scale jitter for the
surviving visual and wrench parts. The exact flip-box values are summarized in Table 3.

E DEPLOYMENT DETAILS
We deploy ForceWM and the IDM in a shared policy service. ForceWM uses one denoising step and
the IDM uses five. Measured separately on the deployment system, their calls take approximately
100ms and60ms, respectively. The reference is refreshed every six actions.
Each refresh caches the generated visual-wrench reference, slow tokens, initial IDM noise, and base
TCP pose used for relative-to-absolute action conversion. Between refreshes, the IDM determinis-
tically re-denoises the action prefix from cached noise using the growing measured-wrench prefix,
executing only its current action token. The GRU re-encodes the available wrench prefix at each call,
and all reference-specific caches reset at the next refresh.
At each reference refresh, ForceWM runs first, followed by the IDM. The previous controller target
remains active during these calls. The first action after a refresh uses the request-time wrench, and
subsequent actions incorporate new measurements. Reference-wrench visibility follows the action
index within the window.
