Perception into Imagination:
Executable Memory for World Models
Xianyao Li, Ruitong Tian, Rui Min, Fang Xu, Jing Du
Abstract
Physical agents must use what they previously saw or
touched after visibility or contact is lost. Their actions
change the implications of these remembered facts. We
introduce executable perceptual memory (EPM), a world-
model framework that advances explicit perceptual state
alongside neural latent dynamics during imagination. Its
evolving read-out informs reward prediction, value estima-
tion and policy learning. We align reward inputs with the
transitions that produced them and supervise the reward
head on open-loop rollouts of recorded actions. On Memory
Maze13×13, EPM reaches 30.37 return at 1M interactions,
compared with 23.37 when the same evolving read-out in-
forms only policy and value on a common perceptual front
end: a gain of 7.00 [3.80, 10.21]. The complete method
also improves control on9×9and in MiniWorld. Probes of
remembered, out-of-view arrivals show stronger reward an-
ticipation in both visual implementations. Concentration,
tactile recovery and real tactile frames demonstrate com-
plementary roles of remembered facts in imagined-policy
training, motion-dependent decisions and sensory-content
retention. EPM connects persistent perception to the pre-
dicted consequences and learning signals of a world model
for Physical AI.
1. Introduction
A robot can lose sight of a landmark when it turns, or lose
tactile contact with an object when it withdraws. What it
previously perceived remains useful, but the implications
change with its actions: the landmark acquires a new bear-
ing, and the remembered contact a new relative position.
For Physical AI, using perception therefore requires both
preserving facts and reasoning about their consequences in
possible futures.
World models provide a setting for this reasoning: they
predict action consequences and train behaviors on imag-
ined trajectories [17–19, 21, 23]. Recurrent states encode
history, while spatial memories and maps maintain explicit
structure [8, 29, 36]. A further question is how maintained
facts enter the futures used for learning: their relations to
the agent change along a rollout, and these changes must
inform predicted rewards and values as well as actions.
We introduceexecutable perceptual memory(EPM). A
perceptual writer maintains explicit state during real inter-
action. At an imagination anchor, a local state is initialized
from that history and advanced using candidate actions and
predicted motion. Its read-out complements the neural la-
tent state in reward prediction, value estimation and policy
learning (Fig. 1). A remembered fact can thus affect both
the action under consideration and the return target used to
learn that action.
The learning interface connects this joint state to the sig-
nals that train behavior. In our DreamerV3 implementation,
a transition-aligned read-out refers to the fact relevant to
each reward, before the next observation writes new evi-
dence or changes the goal. An additional open-loop loss
trains the reward head on prior rollouts of recorded actions.
Together, these inputs and objectives make remembered re-
lations available when learning rewards from real transi-
tions and predicting them in imagination.
EPM improves control over a policy/value-only memory
interface on a common front end in Memory Maze9×9and
13×13, and in MiniWorld. At13×13, it reaches 30.37 re-
turn at 1M interactions, compared with 23.37 for that inter-
face and 14.18 for observation-only memory access. Con-
trolled probes in both visual implementations show stronger
reward anticipation at remembered, out-of-view arrivals.
Comparisons with DreamerV3 and R2I establish the ap-
proach’s competitive context. Concentration, tactile re-
covery and real tactile frames test the same perception–
memory–decision principle through complementary tasks.
We make three contributions. First, we formulate
executable perceptual memory: explicit perceptual state
that candidate actions advance alongside neural world-
model dynamics, with its read-outs available throughout
imagination. Second, we connect these read-outs to re-
ward and actor–critic learning through transition-aligned re-
ward inputs and open-loop supervision. Third, matched-
information comparisons establish control gains and con-
ditional component effects in two visual implementations,
while symbolic and tactile tasks test complementary uses of
persistent facts.

2. Related work
World models and learning through imagination.
World Models [17], PlaNet [18] and Dreamer [19–21] learn
predictive representations for planning or imagined behav-
ior learning. Value Prediction Networks [35], MuZero [42]
and TD-MPC2 [23] emphasize reward and value predic-
tions for control. Dreamer 4 [22], iVideoGPT [44], Nav-
igation World Models [4], V-JEPA 2 [3] and Cosmos [1]
extend predictive modeling toward video and physical in-
teraction. Multi-step objectives shape latent dynamics and
belief states [14, 18]; DreamSmooth studies reward predic-
tion under sparse rewards [28]. EPM connects persistent
perceptual facts to the predicted rewards and values used
for learning.
Memory under partial observability.Transformer poli-
cies [37], structured sequence models [15], S4WM [11] and
R2I [40] strengthen neural memory. Long-context video
models preserve past observations [39, 45, 46]; concur-
rent TT-MBRL encodes episode memory in model parame-
ters at test time [6]. DIACRITIC studies recurrent mem-
ory sufficient to reproduce expert behavior [31]. Cogni-
tive Mapping and Planning [16], Neural Map [36], Active
Neural SLAM [8] and MemoNav [29] organize spatial his-
tory; Memoir retrieves history using imagined futures [47].
GTM-SM couples spatial memory to agent dynamics [12],
and FloWM transforms latent memory with self-motion
and object motion [32]. Concurrent ScanDreamer advances
pose over a frozen map and analytically computes selected
reward and termination signals [13]. EPM instead supplies
executed fact read-outs to a learned reward model, using
transition-aligned and open-loop training. Our comparisons
hold the memory observation common and isolate how its
read-outs enter imagination and learning.
Structured dynamics and grounded prediction.Inter-
action Networks [5], SE3-Nets [7] and structured world
models [27] exploit object and geometric structure. Code
world models [10] and Programmable World Model [25]
express transitions as executable structure. SearchWorld
grounds search in spatial values [26]; DreamLedger uses
execution outcomes to assess reliance on model predic-
tions [30]. EPM specifies how perceptually maintained
facts are executed and read by reward and behavior-learning
interfaces.
Touch as persistent perceptual evidence.Optical tac-
tile sensors recover local geometry and contact informa-
tion [48]. Tactile Gym [33], Taxim [43] and TacSL [2]

3. Executable perceptual memory
3.1. Persistent facts and action-dependent relations
Consider a partially observed process with observationso t,
actionsa t, rewardsy t and an optional goalg t. We represent
history by a neural statez t and explicit perceptual statem t.
The latter stores quantities with defined persistence and up-
date rules: landmark estimates, object-relative poses or ob-
served card identities. At interaction time, their values are
estimated from the agent’s observation stream. Privileged
simulator state is not supplied to the policy.
Real interaction combines state propagation with a per-
ceptual write:
mt =W(C(m t−1, δt), E(ot)), u t =R(m t, gt).(1)
HereEextracts sensory evidence,δ t is measured motion,
Ccarries the stored state, andWincorporates new evi-
dence. The queryRgives a task-relevant read-outu t. A
landmark may remain fixed in world coordinates while its
bearing changes with the agent’s pose. This distinction lets
the model preserve a fact while executing its consequences
under movement.

In navigation,Pextracts the current target’s relative ge-

Perceptual implementation.A landmark detector and
visual odometry maintain sparse target positions and agent
pose. The read-out contains a known flag, sine/cosine bear-
ing, log-distance and log-age. These perception networks
are trained on separate calibration trajectories with simula-
tor labels, then frozen; deployment uses images and actions.
Memory Maze adds image-based heading correction, and
MiniWorld models lateral motion. Every arm encodes and
reconstructs the same memory observation and shares the
same front end within an experiment. Appendix A details
calibration, state updates and storage.
4. Experimental setup
Tasks and training.The principal learned-control tests
use multi-goal Memory Maze [38] at9×9and13×13, and
a MiniWorld [9]9×9implementation with different render-
ing and kinematics. The DreamerV3-based agent uses the
12M-parameter configuration, 16 environments and a 2M-
transition replay capacity. All policies train from scratch.
Memory Maze endpoints are 1M interactions, with twelve
initialization labels at9×9and six at13×13; MiniWorld
uses twelve initialization labels and a 2M endpoint. Each
policy is evaluated on 48 common layout/target/spawn iden-
tities. Episode budgets are 1,000 actions for both9×9
implementations and 3,000 for13×13. Concentration, tac-
tile simulation and real tactile frames test complementary
stages of the framework (Sec. 6).
Six integration choices.Table 1 names arms by their
components. “Held” and “evolved” refer to direct read-outs
during imagination. All arms have the same memory infor-
mation in observation encoding, matched head dimensions
and common initial parameters within a label. Zero-filled
direct slots preserve parameter counts. The six-arm design
is used at9×9and in MiniWorld; the13×13experi-
ment trains the four non-held arms. “Evolved” supplies the
evolving read-out to the policy and value only, without re-
ward access or the open-loop loss.
Comparisons and inference.The full-method contrast
is EPM minus Evolved; reward access is EPM minus
Evolved+OL; OL alone is Evolved+OL minus Evolved.
The held arms measure reward access under holding. Re-
turn differences use paired two-sided 95%tintervals over
initialization labels, with positive-label counts and sign-
flip sensitivity analyses in the appendix. Each task is in-
ferred separately; secondary contrasts are not multiplicity-
adjusted. Budgets and primary endpoints are fixed before
evaluation. Interaction budgets count policy training and
exclude the shared offline perception calibration.
Reward-anticipation probes.Every arm is tested on
common histories from EPM and Evolved+OL policies,
with equal source-policy weight. We compare visible tar-
gets (VIS) with remembered, out-of-view targets whose ar-
rival is anticipated by explicit state (MEM and RA). RA is
evaluated per model, so the retained event subsets can differ.

dicted reward at remembered arrivals and narrows the gap to
visible arrivals, consistent with remembered facts reaching
reward prediction.

6. Remembered facts and physical interaction
The following instances test complementary stages: re-
specting facts during imagined training, carrying state
through lost contact, and retaining real sensory content for
later use.

training

same question. A key–value memory stores observed card
identities. During imagination, a simulator executes the
public game rules while retaining those identities and sam-
pling unknown cards. A matched control uses the same
rules but resamples all identities. The policy is optimized
entirely on imagined rollouts from public-observation pre-
fixes, then deployed in the real game.

6.2. Tactile history supports future branches and
recovery
In Tactile Gym 2.0 [33], an estimator writes object-relative
state from tactile observations while contact is available.
Kinematic updates carry this state after contact is lost and
advance it along candidate actions. On twelve previously
unexposed worlds, explicit evolution reduces pre-contact
prediction error by 0.51 mm [0.28, 0.73] and raises agree-
ment with the best executed branch from 0.02 to 0.64.
We next test recovery choices after a retreat and side-
ways excursion. A chooser evaluates 128 fixed open-loop
paths using an evolved state and a fitted outcome model,
then hands over to a feedback law at re-contact. On 48 new
worlds with varying excursion lengths, it achieves 34.80
mm of progress per recovery window, versus 24.33 mm
for a state-free command selected by excursion side. The
primary difference is 10.47 [8.65, 12.29] mm, positive on
46/48 worlds. It also exceeds a held-action reference by
10.32 [8.64, 12.00] mm and a command table indexed by
side and excursion length by 6.04 [4.43, 7.66] mm (Fig. 4).
A constant-start dead-reckoning reference reaches 35.46
mm; evolved minus this reference is−0.65[−2.02,0.71]
mm and remains unresolved. The results support motion-
carried state for candidate-path selection; this comparison
does not isolate tactile initialization. Appendix I gives pro-
tocols, splits and additional comparisons.
6.3. Real tactile observations survive in compact
memory
Real Tactile MNIST contains GelSight Mini images col-
lected with a robot manipulator [41]. We encode contact
frames and their positions into eight slots with 48 bytes of
payload plus up to 9 bytes of validity and age metadata. A
late query asks for digit identity or material at a held-out
position. Evaluation uses 100 held-out objects; background
subtraction uses a per-round offline background computed
from all 256 touches. Figure 5 shows original training-set
frames and the held-out query results.
After 32 touches, the byte memory achieves mean iden-
tity accuracy 0.417, compared with 0.187 without history
and 0.152 with proprioception and the shared image-derived
write gate; individual-seed values appear in Fig. 5. Remov-
ing content bytes returns accuracy to chance, and larger pay-
loads improve retention. A 128-dimensional, non-quantized
GRU reference reaches 0.485. For material queries, the byte
memory exceeds this control on two of three seeds, with the
third unresolved. Compact memory thus retains useful real
sensory content.
7. Discussion and conclusion
Executable perceptual memory gives world models a way
to use facts that outlast their immediate observation. Per-
ception maintains the facts, candidate actions advance their
local consequences, and reward and behavior-learning in-
terfaces use the resulting state. Control gains in two visual
implementations and improved anticipation of remembered
arrivals support this connection from perceptual history to
imagined learning signals.

A. Implementation and memory accounting
EPM couples explicit perceptual state to neural imagina-
tion, reward prediction and actor–critic learning. Its nav-
igation comparisons share the perception front end across
all arms. An additional policy/value-only comparison uses
visual odometry without heading correction, identified sep-
arately below and in Table 3. This appendix specifies the
state updates, learning objectives, evaluation protocols and
complete results for the reported comparisons.
Real-state update and local projection.The writer uses
a calibrated detector and visual odometry to maintain seen
landmarks and the estimated agent pose. A target query
returns a known flag, cosine and sine of relative bearing,
log-distance, and log-age. Unknown targets gate the geo-
metric slots. The imagination initializer recovers a target
vector from this query, starts the local position and yaw at
zero, and carries the query age. It does not initialize an en-
tire map rollout. For local positionp h, yawψ h and target
vectorv, a predicted translation and rotation produce

The known flag is retained, age advances, and bear-
ing/distance are read fromv rel. The neural model continues
to handle dynamics not represented by this local state. The
motion head predicts displacement and yaw change from
the pre-action latent feature and candidate action; its super-
vision is the subsequent measured motion.
Perception calibration.The detector, odometry and
heading networks are fitted offline using simulator-derived
labels on calibration trajectories, and frozen before pol-
icy training. At deployment they read images and actions.
Memory Maze’s six-color detector uses pooled9×9,11×11
and13×13calibration data, with a visibility half-angle of
0.75 radians; odometry is trained on pooled9×9,11×11
and13×13calibration walks. The heading network learns
(cos 4θ,sin 4θ)from a single64×64frame, exploiting axis-
aligned walls. It uses 87,000 training frames and 15,000
validation frames from separate9×9calibration episodes,
with 30 epochs, batch size 256 and Adam at initial learn-
ing rate10 −3 with cosine decay. After integrating odom-
etry, the writer corrects yaw to the predicted heading on
the nearest90 ◦ branch. No heading correction is applied
inside imagination, where no images are available. Mini-
World uses a separately calibrated detector and a three-
output odometry network for forward, lateral and angular
displacement, trained on calibration layouts excluded from
policy training and evaluation. It uses no heading correc-
tion. Each experiment shares these frozen networks across
arms; the reported interaction budgets exclude this offline
calibration.
Neural paths and parameter accounting.Memory
Maze uses a 31-value memory observation: six target slots,
eighteen detector-stream slots, five read-out slots and two
motion slots. MiniWorld adds lateral motion, giving 32 val-
ues and a three-output motion head. Every arm encodes
and reconstructs the complete observation; absent direct
channels are zero-filled. Reward receives five additional
slots, continuation retains neural input, and actor, critic,
slow critic and replay value consume their assigned read-
outs. The trainable runtime groups contain 12,222,441 pa-
rameters for Memory Maze and 12,223,211 for MiniWorld,
identical across arms within an implementation and exclud-
ing fixed perception networks. The five reward-input slots
account for 1,280 weights, present in every arm. Batches
contain 16 sequences of length 64, the training ratio is 32,
the imagination horizon is 15, and the return horizon is 333.
Gradients from the additional open-loop reward loss update
only the reward head (Fig. 6).
Memory storage.The sparse visual state’s serialized
payload occupies 48 bytes. The neural recurrent state sepa-
rately occupies 8,224 bytes (8,192 for deterministic float32
state and 32 stochastic indices), while visual odometry re-
tains the previous64×64×3image, or 12,288 bytes. Net-
work parameters and replay are additional storage compo-
nents. The real-tactile memory contains 48 payload bytes
and up to 9 bytes of validity and age bookkeeping.

C. Reward-anticipation evaluation
The evaluation filters each recorded episode with real obser-
vations and actions, then rolls the prior along recorded con-
tinuations for 15 steps. Predictions average four stochastic
rollouts. Every tested arm reads common histories from the
EPM and Evolved+OL source policies at the endpoint; esti-
mates aggregate windows within episodes and give the two
sources equal weight. VIS denotes a truly visible target at
the anchor. MEM denotes a non-visible target that is known
in memory, with bearing error at most 0.5 radians. A MEM
event satisfies RA when the stepped read-out anticipates ar-
rival: at the step before the first reward, its predicted dis-
tance is at most the task’s reach distance plus one forward
step (0.2 cells). RA uses each tested model’s predicted mo-
tion, so MEM-and-RA subsets can differ across arms even
on the same histories. The paired contrasts compare con-
ditional anticipation scores, not pointwise errors on iden-
tical events. Simulator truth defines diagnostic strata and
is not policy input. Later rewards after a target switch are
excluded from the first-arrival contrast.

D. MiniWorld implementation
The MiniWorld task uses the maze layout generator, tar-
get identities and action budget of the navigation protocol,
with its own renderer and wall-sliding kinematics. Each
environment maintains an independent scene display list.
Planar odometry includes forward, lateral and angular dis-
placement. All arms use independent training runs from
scratch, matched initialization within each label, and the
same 1,000-action episode budget.
E. Policy/value-only memory interface
This comparison uses six initialization labels, common
initial weights and identical parameter counts across the
evolved, held and observation-only arms. Visual odome-
try without heading correction supplies the same memory
observations to all arms; direct read-outs enter policy and
value heads, and reward training uses posterior neural fea-
tures. Evaluation pairs the same 48 maze identities across
arms and masking modes. Tables 9–11 give all checkpoints
and per-label outcomes. Pairedtintervals and sign-flip sen-
sitivity analyses use training labels as the unit. The primary
sequence tests evolved against observation-only at 500k,
then 1M; other contrasts are secondary and unadjusted.
Masking removes the target read-out from the observa-
tion vector and its direct path while retaining the remaining
observation history and neural state. At 1M, the evolved
arm’s additional masking loss over observation-only is pos-
itive under both the pairedtinterval and sign-flip analysis.
Its dependence contrast against held remains unresolved by
thetinterval (Table 10).
F. Alternative state updates
Advancing age versus geometry.A clock-only compari-
son, on the front end without heading correction, increments
memory age during imagination while holding bearing and
distance at the anchor. Table 12 reports evolved, held and
clock-only arms at all three budgets on six initialization la-
bels and 48 common evaluation mazes. At 1M, evolved ge-
ometry exceeds clock-only on all six labels. The evolved–
held difference is positive on five labels, with a seed-level
bootstrap interval crossing zero. Thus this comparison dis-
tinguishes spatial updates from merely advancing the clock,
while the main six-arm experiments evaluate evolution un-

J. Synthetic pretraining for real tactile mem-
ory
A supplementary experiment tests synthetic pretraining of
the tactile encoder before real-frame learning, using twenty
initialization labels and budgets of 25 and 450 labeled ob-
ject rounds. It uses a causal background computed from
frames observed within the query window, whereas the re-
tention experiment in Sec. 6 uses an offline background. At
budget 25, synthetic tactile pretraining increases 32-touch
identity accuracy over no pretraining by 2.06 [0.70, 3.43]
percentage points, positive on 17/20 labels. Its difference
from Taxim-frame pretraining [43] is 0.45 [−0.74, 1.64]
points. At budget 450, its difference from no pretraining
is 1.35 [−0.54, 3.24] points. Table 15 gives the primary
and secondary comparisons.
