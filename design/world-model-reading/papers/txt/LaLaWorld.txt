with Natural Language Actions

Abstract
This paper shows that JEPA-style latent world models (LWMs) can learn across diverse domains
and data sources by adopting the language-conditioned interface of video generation models.
We establish this approach as an effective and efficient basis for planning with imagination
and downstream policy fine-tuning. To this end, we introduceLaLa-World, aLanguage-
conditionedLatentWorldmodel that learns from simulation and robot trajectories together
with broader image and video data. Two designs make this work. First, we describe both
numerical actions and visual changes in language, enabling joint training of a single future
predictor across heterogeneous datasets. Second, we build the predictor on a language model,
allowing it to interpret language conditions while predicting future latent states. The resulting
predictor achieves higher average MPC planning performance than existing LWMs trained
separately for each domain or task. Adding image and video data further improves average
planning performance and robustness to action phrasing. In a matched comparison with an
RAEv2-based video generation model baseline, LaLa-World reaches comparable planning
performance with 6× less training cost and 13.9× faster planning. Beyond planning, the
pretrained LaLa-World serves as an initialization forLaLa-Policy, which jointly predicts
actions and future visual representations. LaLa-Policy performs competitively with Cosmos
Policy on LIBERO and RoboCasa, with up to5.5×faster inference.

1 Introduction
A central capability of an intelligent agent is to anticipate how the world will change in response
to its actions. Before pushing a cup across a table, we know where it will end up and whether
it might fall. World models capture this capability by predicting future states under candidate
actions, providing a basis for planning or decision-making (Sutton, 1991; Ha & Schmidhuber, 2018;
LeCun, 2022; Hafner et al., 2020). A promising approach to making this predictive capability
computationally efficient is to model how visual representations change under actions, without
learning to reconstruct images at the pixel level. This motivates our focus on JEPA-style latent world
models (LWMs), such as V-JEPA 2-AC (Assran et al., 2025) and DINO-WM (Zhou et al., 2025).1
This efficiency of LWMs is particularly useful for planning with imagination in the latent space,
which requires repeatedly evaluating candidate action sequences, by rolling out multiple candidate
futures at low cost (Hansen et al., 2022). However, despite this advantage, the action-conditioned
predictors in such LWMs have largely been trained on simulation or robotics datasets, where
numerical actions have domain-specific dimensions and physical meanings. For example, [2,4] may
encode horizontal and vertical displacement in one domain and [4] force in another, complicating
the learning of shared dynamics. Furthermore, image and video data, which provide a rich source
of observations of the world, often lack these numerical action annotations, making them difficult
to use for training action-conditioned future predictors. Therefore, scaling LWMs across diverse
control actions and broader image and video data requires a shared interface that accommodates
heterogeneous data.
We suggest that language can serve as the shared interface. We introduce LaLa-World, a language-
conditioned LWM that learns from diverse control datasets and broader image and video data with a
single future predictor. Language conditioning already supports learning from diverse visual data in
video generation (Wan et al., 2025; Agarwal et al., 2025). We use language as a shared interface for
numerical actions and visual changes and can thereby scale LWM training with a single predictor
across diverse contexts and actions.
As illustrated in Figure 1, LaLa-World has two main designs. First, we convert numerical actions in
simulation or robotics datasets into natural language, allowing them to be expressed in the same
form, such as move 2 left and 4 up and push with force 2 downward . We combine these control trajectories
with image and video transitions described in language to help the predictor relate descriptions
to visual changes across diverse scenes. Second, we equip the future predictor with language
understanding. Built on a language model, it interprets language conditions while predicting future
latent states. These designs allow one model to learn from different action spaces and data sources
through a shared prediction objective. Figure 2 shows that a single LaLa-World predictor serves
multiple domains and predicts future states under qualitative actions such as move slightly left .

Our experiments show that our jointly trained predictor achieves higher average MPC planning
performance than existing LWM baselines trained separately for each domain. We also show that
adding image and video data to training further improves planning performance and robustness to
action phrasing. We compare LaLa-World with an RAEv2-based generative world model (Singh
et al., 2026), whose predictor uses a diffusion Transformer (DiT). With the same language conditions,
training data, and visual encoder, LaLa-World outperforms this RAEv2 baseline in planning with
6×less training cost and13.9×faster inference.
Beyond planning, we investigate whether the pretrained LWM can serve as a useful initialization for
policy fine-tuning. Cosmos Policy (Kim et al., 2026) demonstrates this use of a pretrained video
model by adapting Cosmos-Predict2 (Ali et al., 2025) to predict robot actions together with future
frames. Similarly, we fine-tune LaLa-World into LaLa-Policy for joint prediction of actions and
future visualrepresentations. On LIBERO and RoboCasa, LaLa-Policy performs competitively with
Cosmos Policy while reducing average inference time per trajectory by a factor of up to 5.5. We
hope these results draw greater attention to latent world models, showing that even without visually
compelling outputs, they can offer practical advantages over generative world models in planning
and downstream policy learning.
We summarize our contributions as follows:
• We introduce LaLa-World, a latent world model that uses language as a shared interface for joint
training on control trajectories and broader image and video data.
• We show that a single LaLa-World predictor learns dynamics across domains and outperforms
existing LWMs in MPC planning.
• We compare LaLa-World with an RAEv2-based generative world model under matched settings,
demonstrating higher planning performance and greater training and inference efficiency.
• We fine-tune the pretrained LaLa-World into LaLa-Policy, which performs competitively with
existing policy models while achieving faster inference than Cosmos Policy.

2 Background and Motivation
World models aim to predict how an environment evolves under a given condition (Ha & Schmid-
huber, 2018; LeCun, 2022; Wan et al., 2025; Agarwal et al., 2025). One approach to making
future prediction computationally efficient is to predict changes in latent representations. Latent
World Models (LWMs), including V-JEPA 2-AC (Assran et al., 2025) and DINO-WM (Zhou et al.,
2025), follow this approach. These models commonly use numerical actions in the following
action-conditioned formulation:
et =E θ(xt),ˆe t+1 =P ϕ (et−w+1:t, ct−w+1:t), c t ∈R k,(1)
where xt and ct denote the observation and k-dimensional numerical action condition at time
t, respectively, Eθ encodes observations into latent representations, and Pϕ predicts the future
latent ˆet+1 from a context window of length w. The action-conditioned predictors in these models
are commonly trained on simulation or robotics trajectories and evaluated through planning in a
domain-specific manner (Sobal et al., 2025; Maes et al., 2026a;b)
Video generation models (i.e., generative world models) are trained to synthesize images or videos
conditioned on language (Brooks et al., 2024; Wan et al., 2025; Agarwal et al., 2025). This language
interface allows them to learn visual changes across domains and data sources. Through this training,
these models acquire world knowledge that extends their capabilities beyond video generation. Their
pretrained weights, for instance, serve as a prior for action prediction: Cosmos Policy fine-tunes
Cosmos-Predict2 into a robot policy (Kim et al., 2026; Ali et al., 2025).
For LWMs, however, learning jointly from heterogeneous control datasets and broader visual data
remains underexplored, as does their use in downstream policy learning. We therefore explore
an approach that retains the efficiency of LWMs while adopting the flexible language interface
of generative world models. The resulting model supports evaluation against existing LWMs in
planning and against generative world models in both planning and policy fine-tuning.
3 LaLa-World
We introduceLaLa-World, aLanguage-conditionedLatentWorldmodel whose predictor takes a
language condition z instead of the numerical action condition c in Equation (1). Two components
are needed: transition data that describes the change fromxt to xt+1 in language, and a predictor that
understands it. Section 3.1 covers the first, describing how we construct such language-conditioned
data, and Section 3.2 covers the second with the architecture and training objective.
3.1 Language-Conditioned Transition Data
Language unifies the action spaces of multiple domains.Simulation and robotics datasets
used to train LWMs record actions as numerical vectors. We convert these vectors into language
5
conditions using fixed templates, as illustrated in Figure 3, retaining each domain’s numerical values
with their direction and magnitude. Language thus expresses actions across domains in a common
form, allowing their control data to be pooled to train a single predictor. We apply this conversion to
seven simulation and robotics datasets described in Section 4. The actions in these domains may
differ in physical meaning and dimensionality. This raises the key question of whether a single
future predictor can learn dynamics across these domains. Details of the dataset composition and
conversion templates are provided in Section D.1.
Language enables action-conditioned training on broader image and video data.We also
train the same future predictor on image and video transitions without numerical action annotations,
using descriptions of visual change as conditions. Such data matter because the converted control
datasets describe actions with fixed templates, which exposes the predictor to only a narrow range
of language. In addition, descriptions of visual change provide language conditions for these
transitions, extending training beyond the control trajectories. We therefore supplement them with
captioned videos from OpenVid (Nan et al., 2025) and image-editing pairs with instructions from
OmniEdit (Wei et al., 2025) and ShareGPT (Chen et al., 2025b). In these data, the caption describing
the change between frames or the editing instruction serves directly as the language condition, so no
numerical action annotation is needed. The predictor thereby sees a wider range of language and
visual change.
3.2 Model Architecture and Objective
As shown in Figure 1(b), LaLa-World follows the encoder-predictor framework of existing LWMs,
with simple adaptations for language conditioning. Below, we describe the observation encoder (i.e.,
vision encoder), transition predictor (i.e., future predictor), and training objective.
Encoder.LWMs use an observation encoder to map each RGB frame xt to a latent representation
et (Assran et al., 2025; Terver et al., 2025). For this role, vision transformers pretrained with
self-supervised objectives are commonly used, including DINO encoders (Oquab et al., 2024;
Siméoni et al., 2025) and V-JEPA 2 encoder (Assran et al., 2025). These encoders are pretrained
on large collections of unlabeled images and videos, providing visual representations for diverse
environments (Zhou et al., 2025). In V-JEPA 2-AC, the encoder remains frozen during world
model training, which prevents encoder collapse and preserves its pretrained visual representations.
Following JEPA-WMs (Terver et al., 2025), we use DINOv2-S/14 or DINOv3-L/16 as the frozen
observation encoder. More details and ablations on vision encoders are provided in Section C.5.
Predictor.The predictor Pϕ takes the visual contextet−w+1:t and the language conditionz as input
and predicts the next latent representation ˆet+1. Existing world models typically receive conditions
in two ways: LWMs incorporate numerical actions through mechanisms such as AdaLN (Terver
et al., 2025), feature conditioning (Zhou et al., 2025), and sequence conditioning (Nam et al., 2026),
all of which offer no way to read text. Video generation models support language conditioning
through a separate text encoder whose outputs condition the predictor (Wan et al., 2025), which
adds parameters and text-encoding cost.
LaLa-World addresses both limitations by using a pretrained language model as the predictor itself:
it reads the language condition as text and predicts future latent representations in the same network.
Specifically, we use Qwen3.5-0.8B (Qwen Team, 2026) as our default predictor backbone. The
language condition z is tokenized using the model’s tokenizer and mapped to token embeddings,
which are passed to the predictor together with the visual latents. To map the visual latents into the
same embedding space, we use a two-layer MLP and add 3D positional embeddings indexed by
time and 2D spatial location (Wang et al., 2024). The output ˆet+1 is then read out from learned
query tokens (Pan et al., 2025). We train the predictor by minimizing the squared error between the
predicted and target future representations:L=∥ˆe t+1 −e t+1∥2.
Notably, we use proprioceptive state neither as an input nor as a prediction target. While proprio-
ception can benefit LWMs tailored to a specific domain, it is defined in a domain-specific manner,
which limits its applicability to a domain-agnostic world model. We further examine the predictor’s
backbone and architectural choices through ablations in Section C.3 and D.2, respectively, including
BERT (Devlin et al., 2019) and Gemma (Gemma Team, 2025) as alternative backbones.

4 MPC Planning
Suppose we want to move a cup from our hand onto a table while avoiding nearby objects. We
imagine the outcomes of candidate action sequences and select the one that brings the cup closest to
the desired position (i.e., planning with imagination). In robot planning, the world model provides
these imagined outcomes: given an initial observation xinit and a goal observation xgoal, a planner
selects the action sequence whose imagined outcome is closest to the goal. Following prior LWM
studies (Zhou et al., 2025; Terver et al., 2025), we evaluate planning with model predictive control
(MPC), using the Cross-Entropy Method (CEM) to optimize action sequences (Rubinstein, 1997).
Evaluation Setup.At each planning round, we sample candidate action sequences and, for each
sequence, apply Equation (1) autoregressively for H steps and score it by the squared distance
between the final predicted latent state and the encoded goal:
C=∥ˆe t+H −e goal∥2 , e goal =E θ(xgoal).(2)
CEM repeatedly samples sequences and updates the sampling distribution using those with the
lowest costs. For LaLa-World, we verbalize candidate actions using the training templates. In
simulation, we execute the corresponding numerical actions from an initial segment of the selected
sequence. For domains requiring re-planning, we obtain a new observation and repeat this process.
Benchmark-specific settings and evaluation criteria are provided in Section D.4.
We use seven control datasets from DINO-WM and JEPA-WMs (Terver et al., 2025): six simu-
lation datasets–Wall (Zhou et al., 2025), PointMaze (Fu et al., 2020), Push-T (Chi et al., 2023),
MetaWorld (Yu et al., 2020), Rope and Granular (Zhang et al., 2024)–and the real-robot dataset
DROID (Khazatsky et al., 2024). We also evaluate on RoboCasa (Nasiriany et al., 2024), a domain
excluded from training, to assess zero-shot transfer. In addition to existing LWM baselines including
DINO-WM, we also train the world models of IRIS (Micheli et al., 2023), DreamerV3 (Hafner
et al., 2025a), and TD-MPC2 (Hansen et al., 2024) on the same control datasets without reward or
task information, which is the same setup for these baselines in DINO-WM. For a fair comparison,
we train each baseline using its public implementation on the same data and evaluate all under the
same planning settings, including horizonHand none uses proprioceptive states.
Decoder.To visualize and interpret predicted futures, we build a 12-layer transformer decoder
D that maps predicted latents to RGB images: ˆxt+1 =D(ˆet+1). We train the decoder with a
combination of L1 reconstruction and LPIPS perceptual losses (Zhang et al., 2018), following the
decoder training recipe of RAEv2 (Singh et al., 2026). It should be noted that the decoder is not
used during world model training (Section 3.2).

A jointly trained LaLa-World predictor performs well across diverse domains.Table 1 reports

trained within individual domains, LaLa-World trains its predictor jointly across all domains with

highest average score in both benchmark groups in Table 1. On the five simulation tasks in the

4.2 Latent vs. Generative World Models
With language as the action interface, LaLa-World can be compared with generative world models
trained on the same kinds of data. LWMs learn to match future visual latents, whereas generative
world models are trained for pixel-level generation. We ask how these approaches compare in
planning performance and computational cost. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first
comparison of latent and generative world models in planning under matched conditions, including
language conditions and training data, as discussed in Section A.2.
Setup for a matched comparison.We use RAEv2 (Singh et al., 2026) as our generative world
model baseline because it uses DINOv3-L/16 (Siméoni et al., 2025) rather than a V AE as its vision

action interface and receive the same language conditions. We train both models on the same control
trajectories and image and video transitions used to train LaLa-World. The full-depth predictors are
comparable in size: LaLa-World’s predictor and RAEv2’s DiT (Peebles & Xie, 2023) each contain
approximately 0.8B parameters. We initialize RAEv2 from its publicly released pretrained DiT
weights and follow its training implementation, including flow matching (Lipman et al., 2023) and
REPA (Yu et al., 2025). During planning, we use one or five denoising steps per prediction and
compute the cost in Equation (2) between the resulting future representations and the encoded goal.
Further details of RAEv2 are provided in Section D.5.

5 LaLa-Policy: From World Model To Action Predictor
We now ask whether this pretrained LaLa-World provides a useful initialization for policy fine-
tuning. Cosmos Policy (Kim et al., 2026) takes this approach by fine-tuning the pretrained video
generation model Cosmos-Predict2 (Ali et al., 2025) to jointly predict actions and future frames.
LaLa-Policy.Following Cosmos Policy’s fine-tuning procedure, we fine-tune LaLa-World into
LaLa-Policy using policy data to predict an action chunk together with a future latent representation.
We add learned action queries to the predictor Pϕ alongside its queries for future prediction. Given
an observation xt and a task instruction ℓ, the outputs at the action queries provide a sequence of
conditioning features ht:t+N−1 for an action generator Aψ built on a diffusion transformer. We
use the action-head architecture of Isaac-GR00T (Bjorck et al., 2025), which conditions action
generation on vision-language features. Starting from Gaussian noise, Aψ generates the action
chunk through iterative denoising:
(ˆet+N , ht:t+N−1 ) =P ϕ (Eθ(xt), ℓ),
ˆat:t+N−1 =A ψ (ϵ;h t:t+N−1 ), ϵ∼ N(0, I). (3)
Here, N is the number of actions in the chunk, and the future prediction targetsEθ(xt+N ). Modeling
an action distribution allows the policy to represent multiple action sequences for reaching the goal.
Policy fine-tuning setup.We compare LaLa-Policy with policies that predict actions alone (Intelli-
gence et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025b) and models that jointly predict actions and future observations (Li
et al., 2025a; Liang et al., 2025). For a fair comparison with the joint prediction baselines, we
fine-tune Cosmos Policy (Kim et al., 2026) to predict only actions and future observations, without
auxiliary future proprioception or value prediction. LaLa-Policy follows the same prediction setup in
latent space. We fine-tune on LIBERO (Liu et al., 2023) and RoboCasa (Nasiriany et al., 2024), using
demonstration trajectories composed of transitions (xt, at, xt+1) and paired with task instructions.
For RoboCasa, we consider two training sets: 50 human demonstrations or 300 demonstrations
generated with MimicGen per task. For LaLa-Policy, we keep the observation encoder frozen and
jointly train the pretrained predictor and newly added policy modules. The action generator is
trained from scratch. Dataset and training details are provided in Sections D.7 and D.8.
Evaluation Setup.The policy models are evaluated in LIBERO and RoboCasa simulators. At
each step, we execute 16 predicted actions on LIBERO and the first 16 of the 32 predicted actions
on RoboCasa. We report success rates, counting a trial as successful only if it meets the simulator’s
task-specific success criterion within the allowed number of actions. More details are in Section D.9

6 Conclusion
We presented LaLa-World, a latent world model that learns from heterogeneous control trajectories
and broader image and video data through a shared language interface. This enables comparisons
with existing LWMs in MPC planning and generative models in planning and policy fine-tuning.
Despite sharing one predictor across domains, LaLa-World outperforms existing LWMs in average
MPC planning performance. Under matched settings, including training data, it also surpasses the
generative baseline in planning performance and efficiency. Following Cosmos Policy’s use of world
models pretrained on diverse data for policy learning, we fine-tuned LaLa-World into LaLa-Policy.
It outperforms evaluated VLA baselines and achieves substantially faster inference than Cosmos
Policy. These results demonstrate the value of latent world models for planning and policy learning
without training to reconstruct visual details. We hope this work draws greater attention to latent
world models, their scalability, and downstream uses.
For broader practical impact, future work could explore scaling training data and model size,
extending prediction horizons, and evaluating policies on real robots, as discussed in Section B.

A.1 Latent World Models
Latent world models predict future states in a learned representation space rather than in pixels.
Recent work follows the joint-embedding predictive architecture (Assran et al., 2023; 2025). Existing
studies focus on improving latent prediction for planning (Assran et al., 2025) or stabilizing
training (Maes et al., 2026a). In the former direction, PLDM (Sobal et al., 2025) demonstrates
reward-free planning with learned latent dynamics, while DINO-WM (Zhou et al., 2025) shows
the effectiveness of using a frozen DINOv2 vision encoder. V-JEPA 2-AC (Assran et al., 2025)
extends latent planning to real-robot manipulation, and JEPA-WMs (Terver et al., 2025) study
design choices including multistep rollouts. An orthogonal direction addresses training stability:
LeWorldModel (Maes et al., 2026a) trains the vision encoder and predictor end to end, using SIGReg
to prevent representation collapse. AdaJEPA (Wang et al., 2026) further improves robustness by
updating the predictor at test time. These methods typically train action-conditioned predictors within
a single domain, limiting their development into General World Models. Although Newt (Hansen
et al., 2025) learns across multiple domains, its training remains focused on control and game
environments. Joint training of action-conditioned predictors on control trajectories and broader
image and video transitions remains underexplored. We therefore use language to unify actions
across control datasets and co-train them with diverse image and video transitions. Our use of
language as the action interface differs from Dynalang (Lin et al., 2024), which incorporates
language as observations or task descriptions.
A.2 Video Generation Models
Video generation models (i.e., generative world models) predict future observations conditioned on
language (Ho et al., 2022; Blattmann et al., 2023b; Villegas et al., 2023; Kondratyuk et al., 2024).
Language conditioning lets them learn from web-scale video (Brooks et al., 2024; Wan et al., 2025),
17
and the resulting models serve as interactive simulators for games (Bruce et al., 2024), driving (Hu
et al., 2023), and robotics (Yang et al., 2024; Agarwal et al., 2025; Ali et al., 2025). While video
generation models can produce visually compelling content, using them for MPC planning is costly
because evaluating candidate action sequences requires repeatedly synthesizing the future with
visual details. V-JEPA 2-AC and V-JEPA 2.1 (Assran et al., 2025; Mur-Labadia et al., 2026) report
faster planning than video generation baselines (Agarwal et al., 2025; Bar et al., 2025), but these
comparisons differ in action interface, encoder, predictor size, and training data. RAE (Zheng et al.,
2026; Singh et al., 2026) replaces the V AE encoder (Kingma & Welling, 2013) with a vision encoder
pretrained with self-supervision, allowing a generative world model to share its encoder with an
LWM. Using RAEv2 as a generative world model baseline, we compare it with LaLa-World under
matched settings, including language conditioning.
A.3 Planning and Policy Learning with World Models
Planning with learned dynamics models has a long history (Sutton, 1991; Ha & Schmidhuber, 2018;
Watter et al., 2015; Finn & Levine, 2017; Ebert et al., 2018). PlaNet (Hafner et al., 2019) plans
with the Cross-Entropy Method (CEM) in a latent space trained with reconstruction and reward
prediction. Dreamer (Hafner et al., 2020; 2021; 2025a;b) and IRIS (Micheli et al., 2023) instead train
actor-critic policies on imagined rollouts. MuZero (Schrittwieser et al., 2020) and TD-MPC (Hansen
et al., 2022; 2024) learn latent dynamics with reward and value prediction to support tree search and
model predictive control, respectively. More recent work defines a world model more narrowly as
a predictor of future states (Sobal et al., 2025; Assran et al., 2025; Maes et al., 2026a). Planning
with them is performed at test time by sampling-based optimization toward a goal image (i.e., MPC
planning). Our experiments follow this reward-free and goal-conditioned protocol.
A.4 Action Prediction Models
Vision-language-action (VLA) models such as RT-2 (Brohan et al., 2023), OpenVLA (Kim et al.,
2024), π0.5 (Intelligence et al., 2025), UniVLA (Bu et al., 2025), and CogVLA (Li et al., 2025b)
fine-tune vision-language models on large-scale robot demonstrations to map an observation and
an instruction to actions. These models predict actions alone. When humans want to move a cup,
however, they can imagine the action, how the cup will move, and where it will end up. Video-based
policies such as UniPi (Du et al., 2023) and A VDC (Ko et al., 2024) first generate videos of task
execution and then translate them into control actions. Unified video-action models (Li et al., 2025a;
Zhu et al., 2025) instead predict actions and future frames jointly. Building on pretrained video
models, Cosmos Policy (Kim et al., 2026) fine-tunes Cosmos-Predict2 (Ali et al., 2025) to jointly
predict actions, future states, and values. LaLa-Policy follows this recipe but starts from a latent
world model, predicting future representations rather than reconstructing visual details.
A.5 Visual Representation Pretraining
Self-supervised visual encoders provide the representations on which latent world models learn
dynamics (Zhou et al., 2025; Assran et al., 2025). Contrastive and self-distillation methods (He et al.,
2020; Chen et al., 2020; Caron et al., 2021; Oquab et al., 2024; Siméoni et al., 2025) learn invariances
across augmented views, masked image modeling (He et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2022) predicts masked
patches, and JEPAs (Assran et al., 2023; Bardes et al., 2024) predict the representations of masked
regions instead of their pixels. These encoders are conventionally evaluated by probing or fine-tuning
on recognition tasks such as classification, segmentation, and depth estimation. V-JEPA 2 (Assran
et al., 2025) introduced planning as a further evaluation: the pretrained encoder is frozen, an action-
conditioned predictor is trained on top of it from robot trajectories, and the pair is used for MPC
planning toward image goals. We follow the same evaluation protocol as V-JEPA 2, DINO-WM,
and JEPA-WMs (Terver et al., 2025).
18
B Future Directions
LaLa-World shows that a shared language interface enables joint LWM training on heterogeneous
control trajectories and broader image and video data, with benefits for planning and policy fine-
tuning. These results suggest several directions for extending the current approach.
1. Scaling data and model capacity.With a 0.8B predictor, adding image and video data improves
planning performance and robustness to action phrasing (Figure 4). However, our training data
remain smaller in scale than those of video generation models such as Wan (Wan et al., 2025),
and we have not established whether these gains continue at larger scales. Future work should
increase dataset size and predictor capacity separately and together, measuring their effects on
planning performance and downstream policy learning.
2. Learning visual representations end to end.We freeze the vision encoder to prevent repre-
sentation collapse, but this also prevents its representations from adapting to future prediction.
Better world models may require visual representations that capture information useful for
anticipating future states. Jointly training the vision encoder and predictor while preventing
collapse is therefore an important direction for future work.
3. Extending prediction beyond one step.Video generation models produce sequences of future
frames, whereas our predictor is trained with supervision only on the next latent state. Whether
latent world models can learn to generate similarly long sequences through autoregressive
prediction remains an open question. Addressing this would require training on autoregressive
rollouts with losses over multiple future states, rather than only the next state. We leave this
investigation to future work because of the additional training cost, with particular interest in
how prediction quality changes as the rollout length increases.
4. Extending prediction horizons.Following the evaluation protocols of JEPA-WMs and DINO-
WM (Terver et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2025), our MPC experiments use relatively short planning
horizons of at most six steps (Table H). Evaluating longer horizons would help assess whether
the model can anticipate distant consequences and support planning in real-world tasks that
require such foresight. The multi-step supervision discussed above could support this extension.
5. Evaluating on real robots.Our experiments on LIBERO and RoboCasa show that initializing
LaLa-Policy from LaLa-World outperforms existing VLAs, demonstrating the value of a pre-
trained latent world model for policy fine-tuning. While these simulation experiments establish
this benefit, extending the evaluation to real robots is an important next step.
6. Best-of-N planning with LaLa-Policy.LaLa-Policy achieves competitive action prediction
without value supervision. Cosmos Policy (Kim et al., 2026) further learns from policy rollouts
to predict future states and values for candidate action chunks, enabling best-of-N planning. We
consider extending LaLa-Policy to such planning a valuable avenue for future work.
We hope these directions broaden the use of LWMs while retaining their efficiency in planning and
policy learning.

even when each world-model rollout is inexpensive. A natural direction is to let a capable LLM act

reads actions as language, it provides an interface for this interaction. We therefore ask whether an

successful plans.

We keep the target fixed and vary the condition: numerical language, qualitative language without
numbers, reversed direction words in numerical language, and swapped magnitude words in qualita-
tive language (Figure E(a)). Qualitative descriptions preserve direction but coarsen magnitude, so
some loss of accuracy is expected. By contrast, following reversed direction words should move the
prediction away from the original target and can make the score negative. Magnitude swaps preserve
direction but change the amount of motion, so we expect a smaller drop. Panel (b) illustrates this
expected pattern.
Training data.With control data alone, the score drops when numerical magnitudes are replaced
by qualitative descriptions (Figure E(c)). Adding image and video transitions narrows this gap and
makes the direction-swap score more negative. These results suggest that broader transition data
help the predictor interpret qualitative magnitudes and respond to direction words.
Language backbone.We train BERT-0.3B (Devlin et al., 2019), Gemma-0.3B (Gemma Team,
2025), Nemotron-1B (Blakeman et al., 2025), and Qwen3.5-0.8B (Qwen Team, 2026) on the
same data. Qwen3.5 most closely follows the expected sensitivity pattern, while Gemma shows
a similar pattern with lower scores under the original actions (Figure E(d)). Nemotron reaches a
validation loss close to Qwen3.5 (Figure F(a)), yet its score barely changes when direction words
are reversed. Thus, low prediction loss alone does not establish that a predictor follows action
semantics. Pretrained Qwen3.5 also reaches a lower loss earlier in training than its randomly
initialized counterpart, although their final losses are similar.

D.2 LaLa-World Architecture Details
Each example contains observed context frames, language conditions for the corresponding tran-
sitions, and one target frame. In multi-frame training, we sample the context length uniformly
from one to three frames, subject to the available episode history; image pairs provide one context
frame. A two-layer MLP projects each frame’s patch representations into the language model’s
embedding space. As illustrated in Figure G(a), we append one query token per target patch after
the context and language tokens. The queries are copies of a shared learned vector, with their target
locations specified by 3D positional embeddings. These embeddings encode a temporal index and
the row and column of each spatial patch. Context patches share a temporal index within each frame,
while queries receive the target frame’s temporal index and spatial coordinates. We concatenate
sinusoidal encodings for the three axes, multiply them by a learnable scale, and add them to the
input embeddings while retaining the backbone’s native positional encoding. The appended queries
receive the preceding visual and language context through the LLM backbone. A two-layer MLP
prediction head maps their output hidden states back to the vision encoder’s feature dimension,
producing the future patch grid in one forward pass. Both projection MLPs use GELU between their
linear layers. The target frame is used only to provide supervision for these predictions.

D.3 LaLa-World Training Details
We keep the observation encoder frozen and jointly train the language-model backbone, visual
projection layers, and learned query tokens. The same encoder provides the context and target
representations. We optimize the prediction objective in Section 3.2. We use AdamW with an
effective global batch size of 512, (β1, β2) = (0.9,0.98) , and weight decay of 0.05. The learning
rate increases linearly from 1×10 −5 to 2×10 −4 over the first 1,000 optimizer steps, followed
by a cosine decay schedule with a minimum learning rate of 1×10 −6. We clip the gradient norm
at 1.0 and use gradient accumulation to maintain the global batch size. We train for 20 epochs
using bfloat16 mixed precision on eight NVIDIA H100 80GB GPUs. We define each epoch as
approximately one million training examples sampled from the training pool.
D.4 MPC Planning and Evaluation Details
D.4.1 Benchmark Settings and Evaluation Metrics
We follow the planning and evaluation protocols of JEPA-WMs (Terver et al., 2025) and DINO-
WM (Zhou et al., 2025), with our own success criteria for Rope and Granular. As in Section 4, we
evaluate planning with model predictive control (MPC), using the cross-entropy method (CEM) to
optimize action sequences. Table H summarizes the MPC settings, CEM parameters, and evaluation
criteria for each benchmark. One planning step corresponds to one transition predicted by the world
model. In simulation, each action update within a step is called a substep. The planning horizon H
specifies how many steps the model predicts ahead during each candidate rollout, while trajectory
length gives the total step budget.
In simulation, we execute an initial segment of the sequence optimized by CEM, whose length
is specified by executed steps. For benchmarks requiring replanning, we follow the MPC loop:
after executing this segment, we obtain a new observation and re-optimize the action sequence
with CEM. Figure I illustrates these settings. Wall executes all six planned steps in one round,
whereas MetaWorld Reach executes three of six and Rope and Granular execute one of five before
replanning.
For DROID, we evaluate planned actions offline without simulator execution. We compute the
endpoint position error errorxyz in meters from the cumulative planned displacement and report the
Action Score:
Action Score = max (0,800 (0.1−error xyz)).(5)
For zero-shot planning on RoboCasa (Nasiriany et al., 2024), we reuse DROID’s action sampling
statistics because the two domains share the same action definitions and dimensionality.

D.4.2 Action Aggregation and Execution
The baselines represent each planning step by concatenating the actions across its substeps. For
example, five substeps with two action components, x and y, yield a 10-dimensional vector. Verbal-
izing every component would produce a long language condition. We therefore sum each component
across substeps when constructing the training data and verbalize only these sums. In this exam-
ple, the language condition describes two values, ¯x= P5i=1 xi and ¯y= P5i=1 yi, as shown in
Figure J(a).
Within MPC, CEM optimizes action sequences in this aggregated action space, with its initial
sampling statistics computed from the aggregated training data. Each sampled action is verbalized
using the training templates to condition the predictor. To execute a selected action, we divide each
optimized sum by the number of substeps and repeat the resulting action at every substep. For the
five-substep example, we apply (¯x/5,¯y/5)five times, as illustrated in Figure J(b). This preserves
the component sums but discards variation within a step, which can lead to differences between
predicted and executed transitions. It is notable that LaLa-World achieves accurate future prediction
and high planning success rates despite this loss of information.

D.5 RAEv2 Baseline Details
Model and initialization.We initialize the generative world model baseline’s DiT from the
released RAEv2 checkpoint pretrained for image generation on ImageNet.4 Its frozen DINOv3-L/16
encoder aggregates features from seven layers to form the visual representation et =E θ(xt). A
frozen Qwen3-0.6B encodes the language conditionzinto text embeddingsT(z).
Training.For each transition (xt, z, xt+1), we encode the future observation as et+1 =E θ(xt+1)
and interpolate it with Gaussian noise for flow matching (Singh et al., 2026):
e(τ)t+1 = (1−τ)e t+1 +τ ϵ, ϵ∼ N(0, I), τ∈[0,1].(6)
Here, t indexes environment time and τ denotes the noise level. The DiT receives e(τ)t+1, τ, the
current observation representation et, and the text embeddings T(z) . We train it with flow matching
and a representation alignment (REPA) loss (Yu et al., 2025) that aligns intermediate DiT features
with DINOv2-B/14 features of the future observation.
MPC planning.We use the same MPC procedure as LaLa-World, with CEM optimizing candidate
action sequences. Starting from Gaussian noise, the DiT produces a future representation ˆet+1
through denoising, conditioned on the current observation and language action. We evaluate RAEv2
with one and five denoising steps per prediction. CEM scores candidate action sequences using the
mean squared error between the final predicted representation ˆet+H and the encoded goal Eθ(xgoal),
following Equation (2). The goal is encoded with the same DINOv3-L/16 feature aggregation as the
training targets. Pixel decoding is not used to compute the planning cost.

D.6 LaLa-Policy Architecture Details
As illustrated in Figure L, LaLa-Policy extends the predictor of LaLa-World with proprioceptive
input and action queries. Following previous VLAs that use SigLIP-based visual encoders (Black
et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025b), we use SigLIP2-So400M/14 at 224×224 reso-
lution and the 24-layer Qwen3.5-0.8B as the predictor backbone. Each camera view is encoded
independently, and a shared two-layer MLP projects its patch representations into the backbone’s
embedding space. We add learned view embeddings and map the current proprioceptive state to one
token through a separate MLP.
The input sequence contains visual tokens, the proprioceptive token, task instruction tokens, action
queries, and future queries, in this order. We use N= 16 action queries for LIBERO and N= 32
for RoboCasa, with one future query per spatial patch in each camera view. A two-layer MLP maps
the future-query outputs to the encoder’s feature dimension to predict the representations at t+N .
The action generator follows Isaac-GR00T (Bjorck et al., 2025), using 16 DiT blocks of width 1,536
with alternating cross-attention and self-attention. It generates the action chunk from Gaussian noise,
conditioned on the predictor’s full sequence of output hidden states.
D.7 Policy Model Training Data
We describe the datasets used to fine-tune LaLa-Policy in Section 5. The episode counts below refer
to the data before the training/validation split.
LIBERO.We use the Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long suites of LIBERO (Liu et al., 2023),
comprising 40 language-conditioned manipulation tasks. The original data contain 50 human
demonstrations per task, for a total of 2,000 episodes. We adopt the replayed datasets released
by Cosmos Policy (Kim et al., 2026). 6 Its preprocessing removes no-op transitions and filters
unsuccessful replays from the policy demonstration set. The resulting set contains 1,700 successful
episodes: 430, 456, 438, and 376 from Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long, respectively. We use
these episodes for policy training and the full set of 2,000 replays, including failures, for action-
conditioned future prediction.
RoboCasa.We use 24 kitchen manipulation tasks from RoboCasa (Nasiriany et al., 2024) under
two data settings. For the setting with approximately 50 human demonstrations per task, we use the
Cosmos Policy release.7 It provides 1,199 successful episodes for policy training and 1,291 replay
episodes, including 92 failures, for action-conditioned future prediction. For the 300-demonstration
setting, we use RoboCasa’s released data generated with MimicGen (Mandlekar et al., 2023).

D.8 LaLa-Policy Fine-tuning Details
We initialize the predictor from pretrained LaLa-World and jointly train it with the newly added
policy modules, while keeping the observation encoder frozen. The action generator is trained from
scratch. We minimize a flow-matching velocity loss on policy examples and a mean squared error
loss on future representations for both types of examples. Both loss coefficients are set to1.
We use AdamW with an effective global batch size of 256,(β1, β2) = (0.9,0.98) , and weight decay
of 0.05. The learning rate increases linearly from 1×10 −6 to 1×10 −4 over the first 1,000 optimizer
steps, then follows a cosine decay to 1×10 −6. We clip the gradient norm at 1.0 and use bfloat16
mixed precision on eight NVIDIA H100 80GB GPUs, with gradient accumulation to maintain the
global batch size. We train for 8 epochs on LIBERO, 12 epochs on RoboCasa with approximately
50 human demonstrations per task, and 3 epochs on RoboCasa with 300 MimicGen demonstrations
per task.
D.9 Policy Model Evaluation Details
We evaluate LaLa-Policy through direct interaction with the LIBERO (Liu et al., 2023) and Robo-
Casa (Nasiriany et al., 2024) simulators, following the evaluation procedure of Cosmos Policy (Kim
et al., 2026). The policy receives the task instruction, current proprioceptive state, and camera
observations. LIBERO provides an external camera view and a wrist view, while RoboCasa provides
left and right external views and a wrist view.
After 10 initial no-op steps, the policy predicts an action chunk. On LIBERO, the chunk contains 16
actions, all of which are executed before the next query. On RoboCasa, the chunk contains 32 actions,
of which we execute the first 16. Each subsequent query uses the latest simulator observations. The
predicted future representations are not used for action selection during evaluation.
We run 50 episodes for each of the 40 tasks across LIBERO’s Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long
suites. For RoboCasa, we run 50 episodes for each of 24 tasks, distributed equally across five test
scenes with held-out object instances from split B. The step budgets follow the Cosmos Policy
evaluation code: 220, 280, 300, and 520 for the four LIBERO suites, and 300–1,000 depending on
the RoboCasa task. An episode ends upon task success or exhaustion of its step budget. We report
success rates averaged across suites for LIBERO and across tasks for RoboCasa.
