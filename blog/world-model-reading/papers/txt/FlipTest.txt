The Flip Test:
Video World Models Fail to Learn the World State

Video world models promise to predict the future of a scene from its present. Do current models keep
this promise? We probe this question counterfactually. When a small change to the present flips the
future, such as a box resting a few millimeters before or past a table edge, a model that tracks the state
of the world must flip its prediction too. We build FlipTest, a three-level hierarchy of increasingly strict
tests over 1,680 initial frames from 10 simulated and 15 real-world tasks. At the base,visual minimal
pairs, two nearly identical frames whose true futures differ, test whether predictions change across
the boundary; matched controls go further and test whether they change only where the future does;
and dense sweeps, the strictest level, locate exactly where they change. All 11 video world models we
evaluate fall short. The best averages 18.3% pair accuracy over real and simulated scenes, below the
25% of random guessing. Models flip as often on steps that leave the future unchanged as on steps that
change it, and their response stays flat across the sweeps. On the same frames, a vision-language model
is selective and traces a near S-shaped curve near the boundary, and a coding agent that measures the
image comes close to the true boundary. This implies that the deciding information is in the frame, and
video models just miss it. We therefore urge the community to rethink world modeling as pure video
prediction. Next-frame prediction must do more than draw plausible pixels; it must also identify the
causal states that control future dynamics.

1. Introduction
The world is causal.Whether a cup near a table edge falls, a ball passes through a narrow gap, or a
weighted beam tips is already determined in the present by a few millimeters of position. Humans
learn early to read these futures directly from the present state. Infants just a few months old look
longer when a precariously perched box fails to fall (Baillargeon, 2004; Baillargeon et al., 1992);
they expect objects to behave as solid, continuous bodies (Spelke et al., 1992) and actively explore
events that violate these expectations (Stahl and Feigenson, 2015). Craik proposed that the mind
achieves this by building a small-scaleworld modelof external reality to mentally test alternatives
before acting (Craik, 1967). Later work grounded these models in motor control (Wolpert et al.,
1995) and intuitive physics, where dedicated neural circuits (Fischer et al., 2016) run approximate
mental simulations of scenes (Battaglia et al., 2013; Hegarty, 2004; Kubricht et al., 2017; Ullman
et al., 2017). Crucially, an internalworld modelis useful precisely because its forecasts are causally
tied to the present: a critical shift in initial conditions yields a fundamentally different future.
Modern AI aspires to endow machines with this same predictive power. Embodied agents learn
internal dynamics to plan inside latent worlds (Ha and Schmidhuber, 2018; Hafner et al., 2025),
while large-scale video world models aim to simulate the future directly in pixels (Agarwal et al., 2025,
2026; OpenAI, 2024). Trained to extrapolate a scene from its initial frame, today’s video generators
produce photorealistic rollouts and are increasingly championed as general-purpose simulators for

How can we rigorously test whether video world models fulfill this promise? Existing benchmarks
evaluate generated videos in isolation, scoring each rollout for general physical plausibility (Bansal
et al., 2025, 2026; Li et al., 2026; Motamed et al., 2026). However, plausibility is notoriously ill-
defined and requires bespoke, event-specific metrics that are difficult to scale. More importantly,
single-video evaluation has a critical blind spot: a model can hallucinate the exact same convincing
rollout for two scenes whose true futures are opposite, and an isolated plausibility score will never
catch the contradiction. Our key insight is to instead probe world modelscounterfactually—by
perturbing the present just enough to flip the future. By comparing predictions across such tipping
points, we reduce evaluation to a clean, binary outcome label that generalizes across domains
with a sharp physical boundary.

ingly stringent diagnostics (Fig. 1). FlipTest comprises 1,680 initial frames spanning 10 simulated
and 15 real-world tasks, each governed by a sharp physical boundary, such as supported vs. falling,
passing vs. blocked, or stable vs. collapsing.

Why do video world models fail? To diagnose the bottleneck, we decompose visual prediction into
perception, dynamics, and rendering (Wu et al., 2017a) and compare video models against predictors
that isolate or bypass specific stages. Bypassing pixel rendering with a vision-language model (VLM)
that predicts outcomes in text already yields selective transitions at the boundary. A coding agent
that explicitly measures pixel geometry localizes the boundary with high precision, and feeding those
measurements into a physics engine alters only 4 of 761 predictions. The outcome can therefore be
read from the initial frame alone, and once the state is measured, physics is the easy part.
This leaves the video model with two entangled responsibilities: reasoning about the outcome
and rendering the pixels. To isolate rendering, we provide the ground-truth outcome in the prompt.
The best video model surges from 18.3% to 73.7% pair accuracy, with strong gains across all models.
This implies modern video models can oftendrawthe right future, yet cannot deduce from the initial
framewhichfuture will unfold, and even when told, the best model misses over a quarter of the pairs.
We show that today’s video world models have learned how the futurelooks, but not whatdecides
it. Next-frame prediction, as currently formulated, fails to compel models to extract precise physical
state from visual observations. FlipTest makes this fundamental reasoning gap measurable, offering a
rigorous, scalable yardstick to guide the next generation of physically grounded world models.

Minimal pairs and contrast sets.Minimal pairs isolate a single capability by holding everything
else fixed. They have exposed blind spots in grammar with BLiMP (Warstadt et al., 2020), in

1985; Margoni et al., 2024; Téglás et al., 2011) rest on the same logic and compare closely matched

world modeling. It applies the minimal change to theinitial stateand asks for thefuture, so it
probes the model’s transition function directly.
Inverse graphics and physical scene understanding.Inferring hidden scene state from images
dates back to analysis-by-synthesis (Halle and Stevens, 1962; Von Helmholtz, 1867; Yuille and Kersten,
2006). Following this principle, Picture (Kulkarni et al., 2015), Neural Scene De-rendering (Wu et al.,
2017b), and Visual De-animation (Wu et al., 2017a) recover explicit object states from images and
evolve them with a physics engine. Recent work revives this modular paradigm via coding agents that
represent state as code (Chen et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026a), whereas video world
models collapse perception, dynamics, and rendering into one pixel-prediction network. However,
prior work builds predictors along either paradigm without diagnosing which stage fails at a physical
boundary. We repurpose the three-stage decomposition of Visual De-animation (Wu et al., 2017a) as
a diagnostic: comparing video models against a rendering-free VLM and coding agents that measure
geometry or run a physics engine isolates where the decisive state is lost.

3. FlipTest
How can we test whether a model’s prediction is truly grounded in the present state? We first
formalize this counterfactual requirement via boundary minimal pairs (Sec. 3.1), then instantiate it
through a three-level hierarchy of probes (Sec. 3.2), and finally define our evaluation readout
and metrics (Sec. 3.3).
3.1. From Prediction to Minimal Pairs
Let 𝑠 denote the underlying physical state of a scene and𝑦(𝑠) ∈ {0, 1} denote whether a target
future event occurs. Across the physical tasks in FlipTest, the outcome is governed by a scalar or
low-dimensional margin𝑚(𝑠)—such as support overhang, geometric clearance, net torque, or collision
offset—whose sign determines the future:
𝑦(𝑠)=𝕀[𝑚(𝑠)>0],

whether a model is sensitive to this threshold, we construct state pairs(𝑠−,𝑠+) straddling the boundary,

Anymodelthatgenuinelypredictsthefuturefromthepresentmustthereforeassignoppositeoutcomes

model could flip its prediction by chance or in reaction to any pixel change. In Sec. 3.2, we therefore
extend this pairwise formulation into a multi-level hierarchy that tests whether predictions flipif
and only ifthe true outcome changes.
3.2. A Hierarchy of Boundary Tests
To evaluate how faithfully a model tracks the physical boundary, our FlipTest samples states along
𝑚(𝑠) across 1,680 initial frames, organized into three difficulty levels.
Level 1: Pairs (Detection).First, does the model’s prediction change across the boundary?
Each minimal pair places one state on either side of the tipping point (𝑚(𝑠−)< 0 < 𝑚(𝑠+)). The two
initial frames share the exact same scene, objects, and camera, differing only in a single outcome-
controlling variable that flips the ground-truth future (e.g. stay vs. fall, pass vs. blocked). Because the
paired frames are visually matched, solving a pair requires extracting the decisive physical quantity,
such as overhang, clearance, lever arm, or offset, directly from image geometry. Level 1 forms our
core benchmark (Sec. 4.1), comprising300minimal pairs (600initial frames) across25tasks in
six physical families (Fig. 2): statics and toppling, size and fit, friction and rolling, collision and
momentum, free fall and trajectory, and fluids and materials. These are split evenly into 10 simulated
tasks (15 pairs each; 300 images) and 15 real-world tasks (10 pairs each; 300 images).
Specifically, the 10 simulated tasks are built in MuJoCo (Todorov et al., 2012). Within each pair,
the two states sit at symmetric margins±𝛿 around the analytic or empirically calibrated threshold.
Across pairs,𝛿 varies from knife-edge to obvious margins, while camera viewpoints and visual themes
vary across pairs but remain fixed within each pair. Crucially, camera placement depends only on
static scene geometry, ensuring that framing never leaks the outcome.
Complementing simulation, the 15 real-world tasks capture everyday scenes near empirical
physical boundaries. Of these 300 frames, 59 come fromcaptured footagewith recorded physical
outcomes, while 241 arecounterfactual editsof real photographs orstaged synthetic stills. Each paired
frame is constructed via a minimal edit that alters only the boundary variable (e.g. sliding an object
slightly further past a table edge), fixing the ground-truth label by construction while keeping the pair
pixel-matched elsewhere. Section A provides full construction details for all 25 tasks (Tabs. 1 and 2).
Level 2: Controls (Selectivity).Passing Level 1 shows sensitivity, but a model could still flip
indiscriminately at every visible change. To test selectivity, we augment each of the 10 simulated
tasks with 15 control groups (600 frames total), where each group consists of four equally spaced
states at margins−3𝛿,−𝛿,+𝛿,+ 3𝛿 (Fig. 4a). Only the middle step (−𝛿→+𝛿 ) crosses the physical
boundary; the two outer steps apply the exact same spatial perturbation and visual shift while leaving
the true outcome unchanged. A model that genuinely tracks the physical state must flip on the middle
step while holding steady on the outer steps (Sec. 4.2).

Level 3: Sweeps (Localization).Finally, even when a model flips selectively,whereandhow
sharplydoes its belief transition? Across four representative simulated tasks (box at a desk edge,
block on an incline, seesaw, and block stack) with four scenes each, we densely sample 30 states
per scene spanning both sides of the threshold, concentrated near𝑚= 0(Fig. 5a). Across these
480 frames, the ground-truth outcome transitions as a sharp step function at𝑚= 0, allowing us to
measure whether the model’s transition is centered and sharp (Sec. 4.3).
3.3. Outcome Readout and Metrics
To evaluate diverse architectures on equal footing, every model receives only the clean initial frame
and a neutral prompt stating the setup and possible outcomes, without hinting at the underlying
decision rule. While VLMs predict the outcome label directly in text from the initial frame, video world
models generate a video rollout whose outcome must be read out. To do so reliably, we employ two
independent VLM judges, Gemini 3.6 Flash (Google DeepMind, 2026a) and Gemini 3.8 Flash (Google
DeepMind, 2026b), to classify the event depicted in each generated video (or flag it asunclear).
Crucially, the judges evaluate onlywhat the generated video shows, regardless of whether that motion
is physically realistic. On Level 1 pairs, a rollout is scored as correct only if both judges agree on
the ground-truth outcome; inter-judge disagreements andunclear rollouts are scored as incorrect,
since a world model useful for planning must commit to a clear future. For Level 2 controls and
Level 3 sweeps, we retain both judges’ labels to estimate transition statistics (Sec. B details judge
prompts, settings, and inter-annotator agreement).

4. Video Models Fail to Learn Physical State
Models.We evaluate 11 video world models across three categories: general video generators,
including Gemini Omni Flash 1.1 (Google DeepMind, 2026d), MiniMax-H3 (MiniMax, 2026), Wan2.2-
I2V-A14B (Wan et al., 2025) alongside its reasoning-tuned variant VBVR-Wan2.2 (Wang et al., 2026b),
HunyuanVideo-1.5 (Wu et al., 2025), and CogVideoX1.5-5B-I2V (Yang et al., 2025); physical-AI
foundation models, comprising Cosmos-Predict2.5 (Agarwal et al., 2025), Cosmos3-Nano (Agarwal
et al., 2026), and Cosmos3-Super (Agarwal et al., 2026); and robotics-focused predictors, namely
VPP2 (Guo et al., 2026) and LingBot-Video-MoE-30B-A3B (Ma et al., 2026). Each model generates
a single video rollout per initial frame.
4.1.
 Video Models Rarely Solve Minimal Pairs
If a video model truly tracks the underlying physical state, the two frames of a minimal pair must
lead to opposite futures. Figure 3 reveals that they rarely do. On simulated tasks, no model exceeds
5.3% pair accuracy (a ceiling shared by four models), falling far below the 25% expected from
random guessing. On real-world scenes, the strongest model, Gemini Omni Flash 1.1, reaches only
31.3%, followed by MiniMax-H3 (25.3%) and Wan2.2-I2V-A14B (24.7%). Only the top two models
surpass chance, while CogVideoX1.5-5B-I2V drops to 9.3%. Averaged across both splits, overall
accuracy spans a meager 5.7% to 18.3%.
Why do video models perform worse than random guessing? The primary culprit is mode collapse
across the boundary: models overwhelmingly generate theexact sameoutcome for both frames
of a pair, guaranteeing that at least one rollout is wrong (Fig. 6). Pooled across all models on
clearly judged pairs, predictions remain identical across 72% of real pairs and 89% of simulated
pairs. Compounding this insensitivity, generated rollouts frequently violate basic physics. In roughly

4.2.
 Video Models Are Not Selectively Sensitive
Failing Level 1 minimal pairs could stem from two distinct behaviors: either a model is completely
rigid and never flips its prediction, or it flips erratically at any pixel change regardless of the physical
boundary. Our Level 2 controls disentangle these two failure modes. Plotting the crossing flip rate
against the same-side flip rate (Fig. 4b) shows that all 11 video models cluster tightly along the
diagonal: when they do change their prediction, they flip just as often on inconsequential control
steps as on decisive boundary crossings. Consequently, selective sensitivity (Fig. 4c) hovers near
zero for every model, ranging from−0.07(Cosmos-Predict2.5) to+0.07(VBVR-Wan2.2), with every
95% confidence interval straddling zero.

across true physical boundaries.

5. Why Do Video World Models Fail?
To uncover why video world models fail to reason across physical boundaries, we must dissect how
visual prediction works under the hood. Predicting a physical outcome from an image requires
three stages: perceiving the state, evolving the dynamics, and, for pixel generators, rendering the
future. Following Visual De-animation (VDA) (Wu et al., 2017a), which separates these stages into a
perception module, a physics engine, and a graphics engine, we map predictors onto this pipeline to
isolate where boundary reasoning breaks down (Fig. 7a). Whereas a video world model entangles all
three stages inside a single network, alternative predictors isolate or bypass specific stages: a direct
VLM bypasses rendering to predict outcomes in text; a coding agent makes perception explicit by
measuring image geometry with code; and a simulation agent makes dynamics explicit by evolving
those measurements in a physics engine. Conversely, providing the ground-truth outcome in the
video model’s prompt isolates rendering on its own. Evaluating every predictor on the exact same
binary outcome labelˆ𝑦pinpoints which stage loses the decisive physical state.
The frame contains the decisive information.Bypassing pixel rendering with a VLM immediately
confirms that the boundary is visually recoverable from the initial frame. On Level 2 controls, Gemini
3.6 Flash flips its prediction on 61% of crossing steps versus only 15% of same-side steps, achieving
a selective sensitivity of+0.46(95% CI [0.36, 0.56]; Fig. 4), whereas every video model hovers
around zero. Similarly, on Level 3 sweeps, Gemini 4 Argon and Gemini 3.8 Flash trace clear S-shaped
transitions where all video models remain flat (Fig. 5). Initial frames that video models treat as
interchangeable are readily distinguished by a model that reads them directly, though the VLMs’ early,
gradual transitions indicate imprecise perceptual estimation near the threshold.
Told the outcome, video models improve but still fail.If the state is visible in the frame, do
video models fail because they cannot deduce the outcome, or because they cannot render it? To
isolate rendering, we modify each prompt to state the ground-truth outcome explicitly and regenerate
all pair rollouts across all models (Fig. 8; Sec. E). Relieved of deciding the outcome, Gemini Omni
Flash 1.1 surges from 18.3% to 73.7% pair accuracy, with substantial gains across every model.

6. Conclusion
We introduced FlipTest, a benchmark of minimal pairs that asks whether a world model changes its
prediction when a small change to the present flips what happens next. Current video world models
mostly do not. The best solves 31.3% of real pairs and 5.3% of simulated pairs, and models generate
the same outcome for both frames in most pairs. Video models also flip as often within one side of
the boundary as across it, and their predictions stay flat across the boundary sweep. Scaling, newer
versions, physics-specific pretraining, and reasoning tuning barely help.
Our failure analysis traces this gap mainly to reading the physical state and secondarily to
rendering. From the same first frame, VLMs track the boundary and a coding agent that measures
the state solves over 93% of real pairs, while video world models told the ground-truth outcome
still miss over a quarter of them.
This gap matters in the agentic era. Models like Gemini 4 Carbon (Google DeepMind, 2026c) and
Fable 5.2 (Anthropic, 2026) point toward generalist physical agents that operate in real environments,
where trial and error is slow, costly, and fragile. World models promise a virtual sandbox where such
agents can simulate thousands of trials and receive physically grounded feedback before they act. In
this role, basic causality is essential, and current video world models do not yet provide it.

Our results point toward a modular, agentic blueprint for future world simulators. An agent
estimates the physical state from the scene, calls a dedicated simulator or numerical solver when
needed, and passes the predicted trajectory to a video model that renders the motion. This division
builds on inverse graphics (Battaglia et al., 2013; Wu et al., 2017a) and analysis-by-synthesis (Yuille
and Kersten, 2006), as well as recent systems that use code as world representations (Chen et al.,
2026; Liu et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026a). Agents already find the correct outcome, so the open
challenge is to have video models render a given outcome faithfully without overriding the physics. We
discuss limitations in Sec. G. We hope FlipTest helps the community rethink world modeling as pure
video prediction and build world models that learn both how the future looks and what decides it.

A. Task Details
FlipTest has 10 simulated tasks (Tab. 1) and 15 real-world tasks (Tab. 2). Together they form Level 1 of
thehierarchy(300minimalpairs, 600initialframes), andthesimulatedscenesalsosupplythecontrols
and sweeps of Levels 2 and 3. Models see only the clean initial frame. As Fig. 2 shows, the tasks cover
six families of everyday physics:statics and toppling(box at a desk edge, object drop, propped stack,
block stack, seesaws);size and fit(pipe, wall gap, funnel slide, containment by size, bowl stacking,

and momentum(domino cascades, billiards, Newton’s cradle);free fall and trajectory(containment by

Input.All three predictors (the direct VLM, the Measure agent, and the Simulation agent) are

saved as an RGB PNG without metadata under an anonymous file name, together with the scenario
text of the video prompt. This text states the action and lists the outcomes without the camera
instructions, and it names the allowed labels but never the decisive quantity. Ground truth, sample
identifiers, and source paths stay outside the agent’s environment. We run 780 jobs per predictor,

VLM, direct.Gemini 4 Argon answers in a single call without tools, at temperature 0 with a JSON
schema (label, confidence, one-sentence reason). It receives this prompt:“This image is time zero,

Measure and Simulation agents (coding agent).The Measure agent and the Simulation agent
use the same agent, Gemini 4 Argon at medium effort, and differ only in the method that their
prompts prescribe. They run in a command-line coding harness with a shell, file tools, an image
viewer, and a Python environment (NumPy, SciPy, scikit-image, OpenCV, PIL). Both prompts state
that the image shows time zero and that the agent must work only from this image, without searching
for the benchmark, other images, labels, or external implementations.
• The Measure agentwrites a deterministic measurement script,measure.py, reports the key
quantities that it measured, and decides the outcome with its own physical reasoning. Physics
engines and 3D simulation libraries are not available and must not be used.
• The Simulation agentcompletes five steps. (1) It measures the image with code. (2) Before it
builds any model, it writes a pre-simulation judgementpre_sim.json from the measurements
alone and never changes it. (3) It builds the scene from scratch in MuJoCo with a deterministic
build_scene.py and states an assumption for every parameter that it cannot read from the
image. (4) It runs the simulation withsimulate.py, which reads the outcome from the simulated
state in code. (5) It reports that outcome. The agent may declare scenes that MuJoCo cannot
represent (liquids, ink, gels, deformables) not simulable and answer them by reasoning.
Sandbox and audit.Each run executes in an isolated sandbox with no network except the model
API. The agent’s shell cannot reach any other host, API keys are removed from its environment, the
input directory is read-only, and only the output directory is writable. For the Measure agent, an
empty directory covers the installed physics-engine packages such as MuJoCo, so the agent cannot
import them. The harness also provides a server-side web-search tool that the network sandbox
cannot block, so we audit every saved trace. Any call to a web tool, or any network code (HTTP
clients, sockets, package installation, API hosts) in text or files written by the agent, makes the run
invalid. No run used a web tool or failed the audit.
Budget.The main turn has a time limit of 40 minutes for the Measure agent and 45 minutes for the
Simulation agent, and on timeout the agent gets one final five-minute turn to write its best answer.
This timeout happened in 1 run of the Measure agent and 4 runs of the Simulation agent. We retry
infrastructure failures once (5 runs of the Measure agent, 1 of the Simulation agent), and all 780
runs of each agent completed. The median run takes 615s (Measure agent) or 636s (Simulation
agent), uses about 115k tokens (about 28k output), and makes 23–24 tool calls.

F. A Possible Remedy: Contrastive Visual Schemata
Our diagnosis points to a concrete remedy, which we leave to future work. Video models often
generate the same future for both frames of a pair (Sec. 4.1), yet they render the correct outcome
far more often once the prompt states it (Sec. 5). They can draw the right event but fail to identify
which condition in the initial frame decides it. Acontrastive visual schemawould point the model to
27

that condition without giving away the answer. It consists of two matched examples that differ in
one causally relevant condition and lead to opposite outcomes, shown to the model alongside the
target frame. Before the model predicts a ball approaching an opening, for example, the schema
would show a slightly smaller ball that passes through and a slightly larger ball that is blocked. The
examples come from a different scene, so they show which visual difference matters and leave the
outcome of the target for the model to infer. FlipTest already supplies a ready-made library of such
schemata, since every minimal pair is a contrastive schema for its task.
The longer-term goal is a world model that finds the decisive condition without a hand-made
schema for every new situation. Minimal pairs could serve this goal as a training signal that keeps
nearby states with different futures distinct in the model’s representation, so that the model learns
to locate the boundary on its own.
G. Limitations
FlipTest studies threshold dynamics of mostly rigid objects from a single initial frame. We chose
this setting because it makes ground-truth outcomes unambiguous and lets us locate the physical
boundary precisely. One decisive factor changes while the rest stays fixed, so any change in prediction
can be attributed directly to that factor. Real scenes often involve several interacting factors that
unfold over time. Current video models already miss boundaries set by a single factor, and extending
FlipTest to scenes with several intersecting boundaries is a natural next step.
