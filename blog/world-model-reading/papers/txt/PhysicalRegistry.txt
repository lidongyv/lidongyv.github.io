ABSTRACT
Video world models must generate physically plausible futures, not just realistic
frames. Existing approaches typically use semantic, physical, or predictive in-
formation to guide the visual generation stream, leaving a fundamental question:
where inside the generator should predictions of scene evolution live?Standard
video tokens jointly encode appearance and dynamics, but provide no distinct state
for learning and reading predictive dynamics. We answer this question with the
Physical Registry: temporally grounded registry slots inside a video DiT that are
initialized from an observation-conditioned forecast, co-evolve with video tokens,
and receive future-representation supervision, making predictive dynamics an ex-
plicit, readable state of the generator. In a controlled comparison of predictive-state
interfaces on Physics-IQ Verified, Physical Registry improves vanilla fine-tuning
by 9.9/8.7 points on I2V/V2V , the largest gain among the interfaces we compare.
Across all Cosmos and W AN backbones, Physical Registry improves Physics-IQ
Verified scores. With only 0.45% additional parameters, it enables Cosmos 3 Nano
16B to surpass the published I2V score of Cosmos 3 Super 64B (43.56 vs. 42.7).
On WorldModelBench, it also improves physical adherence and the total score
for all four evaluated backbones. Because the registry becomes candidate-specific
while remaining readable, thePhysical Registry Selector (PRS)ranks emerging fu-
tures by how far their intermediate registry readouts depart from the shared forecast
before full denoising is complete. Across four backbones, this selection improves
I2V scores by 5.6–15.9% without completed-video external scoring. Together,
these results support a broader principle:dynamics prediction should live as an
explicit model state that co-evolves with the video representation.Project page:
https://grainsack.github.io/physical-registry/.
1 INTRODUCTION
Video generators are increasingly used as world models, i.e., environments for training agents (Ha &
Schmidhuber, 2018; Bruce et al., 2024; NVIDIA et al., 2025b). From this perspective, a generated
video should contain not only high-quality visuals, but also physically plausible phenomena such as
solid mechanics, fluid dynamics, optics, thermodynamics, and magnetism (Motamed et al., 2026).
However, recent video models still often fail to follow them (Li et al., 2025a; Motamed et al., 2026).
To address this, exploiting physical priors has made some progress by supplying explicit cues
about scene evolution, such as motion, force, or physical control signals (Yang et al., 2025a;
Gillman et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025a). However, it depends on availability of the controls and
corresponding supervision. On the other hand, supervision for aligning the intermediate features
in the video models to pre-trained representations is readily available (Yu et al., 2025; Zhang et al.,
2025; Hwang et al., 2025; Bhowmik et al., 2026). However, their appearance and dynamics can
remain entangled (Bhowmik et al., 2026). This context motivates us to ask a more basic question:
How and where can we internalize scene evolution using representation alignment?

We introduce thePhysical Registry, dedicated registry slots containing scene evolution inside video
DiTs. They are encouraged to be aligned with representations of future frames to be generated,
which are predicted from a source frame. We use JEPA representations for the Physical Registry
because JEPA’s predictive representation learning aligns with our goal of modeling scene evolution:
a frozen JEPA encoder–predictor (Bardes et al., 2024; Assran et al., 2025) produces forecast features
for future temporal positions in the same space as future-video targets, compactly representing how
the scene is expected to evolve. Prior works have also shown that JEPA representations capture
physical properties (Garrido et al., 2025; Joseph et al., 2026). In detail, the Physical Registry of the
first DiT layer starts from JEPA features of the source and future frames via the JEPA encoder and
encoder–predictor, respectively. Then, a readout layer aligns the registries for future frames to the
original representations of the future frames from the JEPA encoder. As a whole, the registries and
video tokens are designed to co-evolve through the transformer blocks such that the registries can
estimate the original representations from the predicted representations.
We show that a dedicated registry combined with predictive supervision yields the largest gain among
the compared interfaces. Notably, this gain generalizes to Cosmos and W AN backbones. Furthermore,
the readouts can be used to choose the best sample among denoising processes from different initial
noises, namelyPhysical Registry Selector (PRS). It works in the middle of denoising processes
instead of completely generated videos (Yuan et al., 2026; Intern`o et al., 2026).
Our contributions are threefold:
• The Physical Registry conveys future dynamics from the initial frames in a video generator.
• The Physical Registry provides an internal selection signal across different initial noises
before fully generating videos.
• The Physical Registry outperforms other interfaces for predictive representations.
2 RELATEDWORK
Physical plausibility in video generation.Prior work improves physical plausibility or controlla-
bility by conditioning video generation on motion, force, trajectory, or language-derived physical
signals (Li et al., 2025b; Gillman et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025a; Yang et al., 2025a; Pathak et al.,
2026). These approaches express the intended evolution through an external control signal, whereas
Physical Registry represents predicted future dynamics as an internal state of the video generator.
Predictive representations inside video generators.I-JEPA learns predictive representations
from visible context, with V-JEPA and V-JEPA 2 extending this principle to video (Assran et al.,
2023; Bardes et al., 2024; Assran et al., 2025). JEPA representations capture dynamics-relevant
structure, including sensitivity to intuitive-physics violations and information about motion and
physical plausibility (Garrido et al., 2025; Joseph et al., 2026). Generative models typically use
such pretrained representations in one of two ways. REPA and its video extensions align them with
intermediate generative features (Yu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Hwang et al., 2025; Bhowmik
et al., 2026; Lian et al., 2026), while ReDi (Kouzelis et al., 2025) and PHANTOM (Shen et al.,
2026) jointly generate visual and representation states. Register tokens offer a different primitive:
Vision Transformer registers provide additional tokens for internal computation (Darcet et al., 2024),
and HyperDiT (He et al., 2026) uses representation-aligned semantic registers for image generation.
Physical Registry brings these lines together by holding forecast-initialized predictive representations
in dedicated, temporally grounded registry slots within the video backbone and supervising them
toward future representations.
Inference-time guidance and candidate selection.WMReward uses V-JEPA 2 as a physics reward
for inference-time guidance and best-of- N selection (Yuan et al., 2026), while GeoPhys uses an
external physical-plausibility verifier to rerank generated candidates (Intern `o et al., 2026). PRS
instead ranks candidates from the generator’s own intermediate registry states, without an external
reward model or verifier.

3.2 PHYSICALREGISTRY
Physical Registry represents the forecast in a fixed set of dedicated registry slots that carry predictive
features alongside the video latent rather than within it. Let Xt ∈R B×C×T v×H×W denote the noisy
video latent. We use the frozen JEPA encoder E and predictor P . The encoder receives a full video
and produces JEPA features for individual frames. The predictor receives the JEPA feature of the
first frame and produces future representations in the shared representation space(Bardes et al., 2024;
Assran et al., 2025). Given an initial observed frame x, we retain the first encoder-derived feature as
an observed context and use the predictor to forecast the remaining features:
H enc =E(x),
J cond = [H enc;P(H enc;n context = 1)]∈ JTr . (3)
We define J cond to be theforecast, with Tr = 8: one encoder-derived feature for the observed context
and seven predictor-generated features for the subsequent future temporal positions. The forecast
occupies Tr positions, calledregistry slots, placed after the video tokens. Each slot is a spatial grid of
registry tokenswhich are the units for attention, whereas slots correspond to frames.
A learned spatio-temporal adapter Gϕ (Appendix A) maps the forecast into the video latent’s shape
and coordinates. At each denoising step t, the same forecast initializes the registry, which is

3.5 THEPHYSICALREGISTRYSELECTOR

becomes a readable internal state that reflects the future dynamics represented by each generation
trajectory. This makes it possible to compare candidate trajectories before generation is complete
using the registry states themselves.

4 EXPERIMENTS
We first show that Physical Registry is a more effective interface for predictive representations than
conditioning and joint generation (Sec. 4.2), and that its gains generalize across video backbones and
world-model benchmarks (Sec. 4.3). We then show that its registry states support candidate selection
during generation (Sec. 4.4), before analyzing the sources of its gains and its responses to physical
changes (Secs. 4.5 and 4.6).
4.1 EXPERIMENTALDETAILS
Benchmarks and tasks.We evaluate on Physics-IQ Verified (R ¨adsch et al., 2026), which scores
physical plausibility against recorded futures, and on WorldModelBench (Li et al., 2025a), which
scores instruction following, physical adherence, and common sense over its full 350-prompt set.
Metric definitions are in Appendix A.
Multi-backbone evaluation.We use Cosmos and W AN 2.2 as two backbone families: Cosmos
is a widely used world-model backbone that supports both I2V and V2V continuation, while W AN

2.2 is a widely adopted video-generation backbone in recent world-model research. Since WAN
does not provide a V2V setting, we evaluate it on I2V only. Models are fine-tuned on the Enlarge
dataset, which combines physics-oriented, web, simulation, and robot videos (Wang et al., 2025b;
2023; NVIDIA, 2026a;b) while excluding Physics-IQ videos (Appendix A). Each backbone–Physical

Candidate selection.All methods use the same ten-candidate pool per input. Plain reports the

intermediate registry readouts, respectively. Published leaderboard values are used for Plain where

4.3 FINE-TUNING ON THEENLARGEDATASET ACROSSVIDEOBACKBONES
Physical Registry improves physical plausibility across backbones.Physical Registry improves
Physics-IQ Verified over every evaluated backbone (Table 2). Under the expanded-prompt setting
(Appendix A), it raises Cosmos 3 Nano 16B from37.3/46.45 to 43.56/49.54 on I2V/V2V , surpassing
the Cosmos 3 Super 64B reference (42.7/48.02) with only 0.45% additional parameters (Table 6).
The WAN TI2V-5B matched-budget control further shows that the gain is not explained by fine-
tuning alone: vanilla fine-tuning on the Enlarge dataset remains near pretrained performance, whereas
Physical Registry improves (Appendix B). Figure 3 further shows more faithful realization of the
intended physical events across five physics categories.
Physical Registry also improves on other physics-related benchmarks.On WorldModelBench,
Physical Registry improves physical adherence and the total score across all four backbones. Com-
mon sense is maintained or improved, while instruction following decreases on Cosmos v2.5 and
WAN TI2V-5B (Table 3; full leaderboard in Table 10, qualitative examples in Appendix I.2). On
EWMBench (Hu et al., 2025), Physical Registry also improves Cosmos 3 Nano’s scene consistency
and all three motion metrics, with the largest gain in trajectory agreement (Appendix E).

4.4 SELECTION FROMREGISTRYREADOUTS
Figure 4 compares PRS with Plain generation and completed-video WMReward. Under the same
WMReward selector, Physical Registry improves over its corresponding backbone across all evaluated
I2V and V2V settings. PRS improves I2V over Plain by5.6%–15.9% across the four default backbone
configurations, using only intermediate registry readouts without completed-video external scoring.
On Cosmos v2.5, PRS selects after 10 of 35 denoising steps and reaches 38.36/46.68 on I2V/V2V ,
slightly exceeding WMReward’s37.74/46.59. Continuing only the selected trajectory requires 125
candidate-steps instead of 350, reducing the serial denoising budget by 64%. Together, these results
show that registry readouts provide a useful early signal for candidate selection before generation is
complete. Appendices F.1 and F.3 provide the full results and early-selection analysis.
4.5 ABLATION OF THEPHYSICALREGISTRY
We examine whether the gains of Physical Registry come from the forecast itself, additional registry
capacity, or predictive supervision. Table 4(a) tests the forecast content, while Table 4(b) isolates the
contributions of registry capacity, forecast initialization, and predictive supervision.
Generation depends on the forecast content.In Table 4(a), removing the projected forecast
drops Nano from 36.01 to 16.78 on I2V , whereas moderate Gaussian perturbation (σ= 0.5 ) has
only a small effect; W AN 14B shows the same trend. The registry therefore depends strongly on the
forecast content while remaining robust to moderate perturbation. In the motion-transfer experiment
(Appendix G), replacing the forecast with JEPA features from a source video shifts the generated
motion toward that source while keeping the initial frame, prompt, and noise fixed.

provides a more localized representation of regions where the intended scene dynamics unfold.
5 DISCUSSION ANDCONCLUSION
Physical Registry gives predictive representations an explicit, temporally grounded state within the
video backbone rather than leaving them entangled with the video representation. Our results suggest
that this dedicated state can be explicitly supervised toward future dynamics while remaining readable
during inference. More broadly, explicit predictive states provide a usefulinterface for representing
and reading physically plausible futuresin world models.

Registry projection architecture.The adapter Gϕ of Equation (4) combines established video-
modeling components: local spatio-temporal convolutions (Tran et al., 2015) and per-position
temporal attention, a factorization used in video diffusion architectures (Ho et al., 2022). The
convolutions mix neighboring locations and adjacent forecast features. Temporal attention then
integrates the slot sequence at each spatial position. Spatial resizing and channel projection match the
backbone’s latent geometry while retaining the forecast’s temporal indexing. Figure 6 shows this
adapter.

format of the backbone:16channels over8registry slots at the88×160latent resolution.
Projection stages.Figure 6 gives the operations and shapes of each stage. Upsampling acts on the
two spatial axes only, and the temporal Transformer is applied independently at each spatial position
with the eight registry slots as its sequence, so spatial mixing comes from the convolutions and, after
concatenation, from the backbone’s self-attention. The adapter is initialized randomly and trained
jointly with the backbone.
Projection placement.The module emits the backbone’s native latent channel count. Its output
is normalized with the tokenizer’s own per-frame statistics for the registry slots, which places the
registry on the scale of the video latent. The normalized output is then concatenated along the
temporal axis to give Tv+Tr = 24 + 8 = 32 latent frames. Registry slot i receives the video-latent
RoPE coordinate 3i, the temporal alignment of Equation (5). With 2×2 spatial patchification,
each registry slot contains 44×80 tokens, giving 8×44×80 = 28,160 registry tokens in this
configuration.

A.4 EVALUATION ANDVISUALIZATIONDETAILS
Benchmark metrics.Physics-IQ Verified measures physical plausibility against a recorded future,
with higher scores indicating better results (R¨adsch et al., 2026). It normalizes four subscores per
sample and averages equally across subscores and inputs, replacing dataset-level normalization. The
input-level scores support within-input candidate comparisons. WorldModelBench (Li et al., 2025a)
supplies a first-frame image and text instruction for 350 prompts spanning 7 application domains
and 56 subdomains. Its human-aligned multimodal judge scores instruction following, physical
adherence over five physical laws, and common sense over frame-wise and temporal quality. The
total is the sum of these scores. EWMBench (Hu et al., 2025) evaluates embodied world models on
real robot manipulation episodes, scoring visual scene consistency, motion correctness against the
recorded trajectory, and semantic alignment with the task instruction (Appendix E). Each backbone
and Physical Registry pair uses the same conditioning inputs, sampling protocol, and judge on the
full set.
Tasks and candidate pools.The evaluation covers image-to-video and video-to-video generation
on Physics-IQ (Motamed et al., 2026). Candidates are compared within the same scene, view, and
conditioning input. Within matched pools of ten candidates per input, Plain scores average the pool,
while WMReward and PRS select one candidate from it. The content ablations in Table 4(a) use
Cosmos 3 Nano 16B without prompt expansion and WAN 2.2 I2V-A14B. They either replace the
18
Preprint
projected registry features with zeros (zero mask) or apply Gaussian noise with σ= 0.5 (perturbed
mask). Their full-model references repeat the Plain values in Table 2.
Controlled comparison (Table 1).All interfaces share the pretrained backbone, center-view
training data, training budget, JEPA source, and held-out left/right-view evaluation. REPA aligns
ordinary denoising hidden states to JEPA features; joint generation adapts ReDi (Kouzelis et al., 2025)
to denoise video and future JEPA tokens in one sequence; Registry only keeps the forecast-initialized
registry but removes its alignment loss. Training uses only center views, while evaluation uses the left
and right views. This protocol is meaningful only if the JEPA condition describes the same scenario
across viewpoints. Figure 8 confirms this: JEPA features from different views of the same scenario
are closer than those of different scenarios in both L2 distance and cosine similarity. A held-out view
therefore supplies a JEPA condition consistent with its scenario, and the comparison tests how each
interface uses this condition under a new viewpoint.

G.1 SETUP AND SCOPE
To test whether registry content can influence generated motion, we replace the native forecast of a
receiver scene (the white-sphere scene) with source-video JEPA features on Cosmos 3 Nano 16B

Experimental setup.The native-forecast configuration combines one source-context feature with
seven predicted features. The transfer configuration instead uses eight features from a frozen
JEPA video encoder applied to the source video. Both representations have shape 8×196×5632
and enter the same trained projection and video-generation interface. The source features are
full video representations, containing scene content as well as dynamics. For this comparison,
both configurations use the same factor-two amplification of the projected registry residual in
the conditional branch, P(0) + 2[P(J)−P(0)] . The unconditional branch uses P(J) without
amplification. Here P is the trained projection and J the chosen registry input. Both receiver outputs
use the same inference configuration: 50 denoising steps, classifier-free guidance scale 3.0, flow shift
4.75, and1280×720outputs with 121 frames at 24 fps.
