DiffW AM: Learning World Action Model from Consecutive Visual Difference
Abstract
World Action Models (WAMs) have emerged as an impor-
tant paradigm in robot learning, jointly predicting future
scene states and robot actions from current observations and
instructions. However, future states entangle task-related
changes with large amounts of task-irrelevant content, such
as static backgrounds, which weakens the model’s ability to
capture changes relevant to task execution. To address this,
we propose Difference World Action Model (DiffW AM), a
Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) framework that enhances world
representations by jointly learning future-state prediction
and consecutive visual difference prediction. We define con-
secutive visual differences as differences between the latent
representations of consecutive observations, providing an
explicit learning signal for temporal variation. The frame-
work comprises a World Expert for future frame prediction,
an Action Expert for action generation, and a Diff Expert
for predicting these latent differences along the trajectory. A
structured unidirectional attention mask conditions the Diff,
World, and Action Experts solely on the current-frame rep-
resentation while blocking attention between experts. This
design allows both the World and Diff Experts to be removed
during inference, introducing no additional inference over-
head. DiffWAM achieves state-of-the-art performance on
the LIBERO and RoboTwin 2.0 benchmarks and substan-
tially improves over FastW AM on real-world manipulation
tasks. Specifically, it attains average success rates of 98.8%,
92.65%, and 88.3%, outperforming the state-of-the-art base-
line (i.e., FastWAM) by 1.75, 1.55, and 15.0 percentage
points, respectively.
1 Introduction
Learning task-related representations is crucial for robotic
manipulation, as a robot must understand scene content and
changes relevant to task execution. Two main paradigms
have emerged to this end. Vision Language Action mod-
els (VLAs) [ 1, 2] map observations and instructions di-
rectly to actions, which is simple and efficient but lacks
explicit modeling of scene evolution during task execu-
tion [3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. In contrast, World Action Models
(WAMs) [10, 11], building on the predictive world model-
ing paradigm [12], learn by predicting future scene states
alongside robot actions, thereby acquiring a predictive un-
derstanding of task-related scene dynamics [13, 14, 15, 16].
Despite recent progress in WAMs [17, 18, 19, 20, 21], a
key question remains:is future-state prediction alone suffi-
cient to learn world representations that capture task-related
changes?As illustrated in Figure 1, much of the scene con-
tent persists across consecutive observations, while task ex-
ecution involves changes such as robot motion and object
interactions. The full-state prediction objective supervises
these changes together with persistent content, without pro-
viding a separate learning signal for temporal variation. Thus,
the representation of task-related changes relies on what
the model learns implicitly from predicting complete future
states.
This observation suggests a principle of visual foresight: a
W AM should model not only what the future scene will look
like, but also how it changes over time. Future-state predic-
tion captures scene structure, object appearance, and spatial
relationships, while consecutive visual difference prediction
provides a separate target for temporal variation. We define
aconsecutive visual differenceas the difference between
the latent representations of two consecutive observations.
Predicting this quantity, also referred to asdiff prediction,
provides explicit supervision for temporal variation without
requiring additional motion annotations. By jointly modeling
future states and their consecutive visual differences, a W AM
can develop a more comprehensive world representation that
integrates scene understanding with task-related dynamics
for action generation.
In this paper, we propose DiffW AM, a Mixture-of-Experts
(MoE) framework that jointly learns future-state prediction,
visual difference prediction, and action generation. A shared
Observation Encoder encodes the current visual observation
into world representations, which are used by a World Expert
to predict future states, a Diff Expert to predict differences
between consecutive frame latents, and an Action Expert
to generate actions. A structured unidirectional attention
mask allows each expert to attend to the current-observation
representations while blocking attention between experts.
This architecture integrates state and visual difference mod-
eling into the world representations used for action gener-
ation, while keeping the prediction pathways separate. At
inference, both the World and Diff Experts are removed,
retaining only the Observation Encoder and Action Expert
without additional inference overhead. Across LIBERO [22],
RoboTwin 2.0 [23], and real-world manipulation tasks, Dif-
fWAM achieves average success rates of 98.8%, 92.65%,
and 88.3%, respectively, outperforming FastWAM [15] by
1.75, 1.55, and 15.0 percentage points.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• We introduce joint future-state and visual difference
modeling in W AMs, enabling world representations to
capture both scene content and task-related dynamics
for action generation.

2 Related Work
Existing embodied policy models can be broadly grouped
into three paradigms according to their model outputs and
supervision: VLA, W AM with Future Prediction, and W AM
with Structured Signal Prediction. We detail these three
paradigms below.
2.1 VLA
Vision-Language-Action models directly generate actions
from visual observations and language instructions, learn-
ing representations for action generation without an explicit
future-state prediction objective. This paradigm formulates
the problem as learning a conditional action policy:
πθ(a1:h |o 0, l),(1)
where o0 denotes the current observation, l the language
instruction, and a1:h the action sequence over a horizon of h
steps. Such policies typically couple a VLM backbone with
an action expert, directly decoding actions from observation
and instruction [24, 25].
Representative works following this paradigm include RT-
2 [4], OpenVLA [5], π0 [7], Octo [26], Diffusion Policy [27],
RDT-1B [28], and Xiaomi-Robotics-0 [29]. These methods
directly map observations to actions, and VLM-based ap-
proaches can benefit from pretrained semantic representa-
tions. However, learning directly from demonstrations may
also capture spurious correlations, potentially contributing
to compounding errors and reduced generalization [30].
2.2 W AM with Future Prediction
W AMs with future prediction jointly model future visual
states and actions, extending action generation with an ex-
plicit representation of scene evolution. Conditioned on the
current observation and language instruction, they model the
joint distribution:
pθ(o1:h, a1:h |o 0, l),(2)
where o1:h denotes the predicted future frames and a1:h the
corresponding action sequence. Rather than modeling en-
vironmental dynamics implicitly, these methods explicitly
predict future frames to ground action generation [31, 32].
Representative works following this paradigm include
Motus [13], LingBot-V A [14], Fast-W AM [15], GigaWorld-
Policy [33], IRASim [34], VideoVLA [35], Video Prediction
Policy [36], and WorldVLA [37]. Future visual prediction
connects scene evolution with action generation [ 38, 39].
Nevertheless, accurately predicting temporally coherent fu-
ture frames in high-dimensional pixel or latent space remains
challenging. For methods that condition action decoding di-
rectly on predicted future states, prediction errors may prop-
agate into action generation [40]. Moreover, predicting com-
plete future states does not explicitly distinguish persistent
scene content from inter-frame visual differences, leaving
temporal variation to be learned implicitly within the state
prediction objective. This can make it difficult to emphasize
task-related changes over visually salient but task-irrelevant
details [41].
2.3 W AM with Structured Signal Prediction
Beyond future-state prediction, some WAMs incorpo-
rate structured prediction targets, such as depth, semantic
segmentation, optical flow, or pretrained visual features, to
model additional aspects of the scene. Formally, such meth-
ods introduce a structured representationsand model
pθ(o1:h, s, a1:h |o 0, l),(3)
where o1:h denotes the predicted future frames, a1:h the cor-
responding actions, and s an auxiliary structured signal, such
as a depth map, semantic segmentation, optical flow [16, 42],
or a pretrained feature field (e.g., DINOv3 features [ 43]).
These targets capture geometric, semantic, or motion-related
information, depending on the chosen signal. By supplement-
ing future-state prediction with such structure, these methods
introduce additional inductive biases into visual modeling.
Representative works include X-WAM [44], AIM [45],
AGRA [46], DeFI [47], and MoLA [48]. These approaches
specify particular geometric, semantic, or motion-related
quantities as prediction targets. Their modeling emphasis
therefore depends on the chosen signal, and targets obtained
from external estimators may contain estimation errors. In

3 Method
3.1 Problem Formulation
As illustrated in Figure 2, DiffW AM jointly models future
states, inter-frame visual differences, and actions conditioned
on the current observation o0 and a language instruction l.
Let fi denote the V AE latent representation of observation
oi, T the number of future visual states, and H the action
chunk length. The joint modeling objective is expressed in
latent space as:
pθ(f1:T ,∆f 1:T , a1:H |f 0, l),(4)
where f1:T denotes the future visual latents, a1:H the action
chunk, and ∆f1:T the consecutive visual differences rep-
resented in latent space (labeledFuture Diffin Figure 2),
defined as
∆fi =f i −f i−1, i= 1, . . . , T.(5)
We usevisual difference predictionfor this auxiliary task
anddiff predictionas its shorthand; the Diff Expert predicts
the latent differences ∆fi. Future-state prediction captures

scene content, while visual difference prediction explicitly
supervises inter-frame variation. Jointly learning these com-
plementary prediction tasks shapes the current-observation
representation used for action generation. The joint objective
does not require the Action Expert to consume predicted
future states or latent differences; each expert is conditioned
on the current-observation representation and language in-
struction. Latent differences describe visual variation and
may also include nuisance changes, rather than exclusively
encoding changes caused by robot actions.
3.2 Model Architecture
Overview.DiffWAM jointly learns future-state predic-
tion, visual difference prediction, and action generation to
enhance world representations. During training, the World
and Diff Experts model future states and latent differences,
respectively, while the Action Expert learns to generate ac-
tions from the current-observation representation. During
inference, both the World and Diff Experts are removed,
retaining only the Observation Encoder and Action Expert
without additional inference overhead.
Architecture.As illustrated in Figure 2, DiffWAM is a
Mixture-of-Experts framework with a shared Observation
Encoder and dedicated World, Diff, and Action Experts. The
Observation Encoder and each expert containN Transformer
layers. The World and Diff Experts share parameters in their
first N−M layers and use independent parameters in their
last M layers. The Observation Encoder maps the current
visual latents into world representations that condition all
three experts. The World Expert predicts future states, the
Diff Expert predicts inter-frame latent differences, and the
Action Expert generates the action chunk. A structured at-
tention mask separates the expert pathways while allowing
each expert to access the current-observation representation.
Parameter sharing between the World and Diff Experts does
not introduce cross-attention between their independent pre-
diction pathways.
Input Encoding.DiffWAM inherits the pretrained text
encoder and video V AE from Wan2.2-5B [ 49]. The task
instruction l is encoded with the T5 text encoder [ 50] and
provides language conditioning through cross-attention. Im-
ages from multiple camera views are concatenated before
being mapped into visual latents by the pretrained V AE [51].
The clean current-frame latents f0 are processed by the Ob-
servation Encoder to provide the shared conditioning repre-
sentation. During training, both the World and Diff Experts
receive the same noised future-frame latents as input. The
World Expert learns future-state generation through denois-
ing, while the Diff Expert directly regresses latent differ-
ences, with targets computed from consecutive ground-truth
visual latents. Actions a1:H are separately noised and em-
bedded for the Action Expert.
World Expert.The World Expert models future states
conditioned on the current-observation representation sup-
plied by the Observation Encoder and the language instruc-
tion. Noised future-frame latents are processed by the first
N−M layers shared with the Diff Expert, followed by M
independent state-prediction layers. The World Expert learns
the velocity field used for future-state generation through
flow matching. Its prediction pathway is used during train-
ing and is removed at inference; the Observation Encoder is
retained to provide the representation for action generation.
Diff Expert.The Diff Expert processes the same noised
future-frame latents through the shared first N−M lay-
ers, followed by an independent M-layer Latent Diff Block
(LDB). Conditioned on the current-observation represen-
tation and language instruction, it directly regresses inter-
frame latent differences rather than generating future states
through denoising. Its independent pathway does not attend
to the World or Action Expert’s tokens, and its outputs are
not passed to these experts. The regression targets are dif-
ferences between consecutive ground-truth frame latents,
including the transition from the current observation to the
first future state. Visual difference modeling influences the
shared representation during training, while the Diff Expert
is removed at inference.
Action Expert.The Action Expert generates the action
chunk through flow matching, conditioned on the current-
observation representation supplied by the Observation En-
coder and the language instruction. It does not directly re-
ceive predicted future states or diff outputs. State and visual
difference modeling improve the representation used by the
action pathway through joint training. At inference, the Ac-
tion Expert operates with the Observation Encoder after both
visual prediction experts have been removed.
Structured Attention Mask.As illustrated in Figure 3,
a structured unidirectional attention mask controls informa-
tion flow between the current-observation pathway and the
experts. Current-observation tokens do not attend to future-
state, diff, or action tokens. Each expert may attend to the
current-observation representation and tokens within its own
pathway, while attention between different expert pathways
is blocked. These restrictions concern interactions between
visual and action token groups; language conditioning is
supplied separately through cross-attention.
(a)In the first N−M layers, the visual and action path-
ways follow a first-frame-conditioned attention scheme [15]:
current-frame tokens attend to themselves, noised future-

frame tokens attend to the current-frame representation and
their own tokens, and action tokens attend to the current-
frame representation and their own tokens. Future-frame and
action tokens do not attend to each other. The World and
Diff Experts share the visual processing layers at this depth.
(b)In the last M layers, the World and Diff Experts use

current-frame representation and their own tokens, while the

(a). Blocking cross-attention between these pathways pre-

entering their prediction pathways. This restriction does not
exclude the noised future-frame input shared by the World
and Diff Experts.
(c)At inference, only the current-observation and action
pathways are retained. The Action Expert generates a1:H
from the current-observation representation and language
instruction without invoking future-state or diff prediction,
introducing no additional overhead from the visual predic-
tion experts.

DiffWAM-S for small-scale training and DiffWAM-L for
large-scale training. Both configurations use the architecture
and joint objective described above, with different parameter
update schedules. Figure 4 illustrates these configurations
and their shared inference pathway.

dependent M-layer LDB of the Diff Expert while jointly
optimizing the Observation Encoder, World Expert, and Ac-
tion Expert, including the visual layers shared by the World
and Diff Experts. Freezing the LDB helps reduce overfit-
ting to noise in small datasets while accelerating training by

introduce no additional inference overhead. The two con-
figurations therefore differ in their training schedules while
retaining the same inference architecture.
4 Experiments
4.1 Implementation Details
The Observation Encoder and the World, Diff, and Ac-
tion Experts each contain N Transformer layers. The World
and Diff Experts use the Wan2.2-5B Transformer architec-
ture [49], with shared parameters in the first N−M layers
and separate parameters in the last M layers. The Action
Expert follows the same architecture but uses a reduced hid-
den dimension of da = 1024. The complete model contains
approximately 7B parameters. Further architectural, opti-
mization, and inference details are provided in Appendix.
The additional clean-to-random study starts task training
without a task-trained FastW AM checkpoint. Separately, the
continued-training control initializes both methods from the
same task-trained FastW AM checkpoint and trains each for
one additional epoch on clean demonstrations. We distin-
guish these two initialization protocols throughout the analy-
sis.
4.2 Experiment Setup
We evaluate DiffWAM on LIBERO [ 22], RoboTwin
2.0 [ 23], and three real-world manipulation tasks on the
ALOHA-AgileX platform. LIBERO covers 40 single-arm
tasks across four suites, while RoboTwin 2.0 evaluates 50
bimanual tasks under clean and randomized conditions. We
conduct 100 evaluation rollouts per task for LIBERO and the
full-data RoboTwin 2.0 evaluation. For the additional clean-
to-random evaluation, we evaluate each DiffWAM policy
over 30 rollouts per task in each condition. For the real-world

Figure 6 compares the training dynamics of DiffW AM and

informative learning signal, enabling the policy to acquire
task-relevant representations with fewer optimization steps
while also improving its asymptotic performance.

Figure 7 visualizes the attention responses of DiffW AM
from the head and wrist camera views. The attention maps
retain fine-grained scene texture, while assigning stronger
responses to the objects being manipulated. This indicates

objects. Overall, the visualization supports our design goal
of learning representations that balance rich scene under-
standing with focused perception of the manipulated object.
5 Conclusion
In this work, we proposed DiffWAM, a World Action
Model that enhances world representations by jointly learn-
ing future-state prediction, visual difference prediction, and
action generation. Its Mixture-of-Experts architecture com-
bines a shared Observation Encoder with dedicated World,
Diff, and Action Experts. A structured unidirectional at-
tention mask allows each expert to access the current-
observation representation while separating their prediction
pathways. At inference, the World and Diff Experts are re-
moved, retaining only the Observation Encoder and Action
Expert without additional inference overhead.
Experiments on LIBERO, RoboTwin 2.0, and real-world
manipulation demonstrate the effectiveness of joint state
and visual difference modeling. DiffWAM-S achieves av-
erage success rates of 98.8% on LIBERO and 88.3% on
the real-world tasks, while DiffWAM-L achieves 92.65%
on RoboTwin 2.0. Comparisons across data settings favor
DiffWAM-S for smaller training sets and DiffWAM-L for
the full RoboTwin training set, supporting the choice of
training configuration according to data availability. An
additional clean-to-random evaluation on RoboTwin 2.0
improves success from 1.50% to 5.27%, and a separate
matched checkpoint-continuation control improves clean
success from 88.47% to 93.13%. Ablation studies further
confirm the complementary roles of the World and Diff Ex-
perts. Future work will explore large-scale pretraining and
extend state and visual difference modeling to more diverse
manipulation settings.

LIBERO
LIBERO [ 22] consists of four suites: LIBERO-Spatial,
LIBERO-Object, LIBERO-Goal, and LIBERO-Long. Each
suite contains 10 tasks and provides 500 expert demonstra-
tions, resulting in 40 tasks and 2,000 demonstrations in total.
We conduct 100 rollouts per task, amounting to 4,000 evalu-
ation trials, and report the average success rate for each suite
and across all four suites. For this benchmark, DiffWAM-
S is initialized from Wan2.2-5B and trained for 1.3×10 4
steps with a learning rate of 1×10 −4 and a batch size of
8. The model uses two camera views, a 7-dimensional ac-
tion space, an 8-dimensional proprioceptive input, min/max
normalization, and network depths of N= 30 and M= 10 .
RoboTwin 2.0
RoboTwin 2.0 [23] evaluates bimanual manipulation under
both clean and heavily randomized environments. We adopt
its multi-task setting with 50 tasks and use approximately
27k demonstration trajectories in total, comprising 2,500
demonstrations from clean scenes and 25,000 demonstra-
tions with strong scene randomization. Each task is evaluated
over 100 trials under both clean and randomized conditions,
and we report the success rates for the two settings and
their average. Due to computational resource constraints,
DiffWAM-L is initialized from FastWAM for this bench-
mark and trained for 3×10 4 steps with a learning rate of
1×10 −5 and a batch size of 8. The model uses three camera
views, a 14-dimensional action space, a 14-dimensional pro-
prioceptive input, z-score normalization, and network depths
ofN= 30andM= 10.
Clean-to-random evaluation
The additional experiment in Table 3 uses clean demon-
strations only during task training and evaluates the result-
ing policies under randomized RoboTwin 2.0 conditions
without adaptation. These runs start task training without
loading a task-trained FastW AM checkpoint; training from
scratch here refers to robot-task training and does not im-
ply that all model parameters are randomly initialized. This
experiment is distinct from the full-data training configu-
ration above, which includes randomized demonstrations.
Clean/Random success is 70.2%/1.50% for our FastWAM
baseline and 69.7%/5.27% for DiffW AM. We evaluate Dif-
fWAM over 30 trials per task in each condition, totaling
1,500 trials per condition. It succeeds in 1,045 clean tri-
als and 79 randomized trials, yielding unrounded success
rates of 1,045/1,500 and 79/1,500. The Clean/Random aver-
age is computed before rounding as 1,124/3,000, or 37.47%
when rounded to two decimal places. The corresponding
FastWAM average is 35.85%. Clean results from the sepa-
rate checkpoint-continuation experiment are not paired with
these values.
Checkpoint-continuation control
For Table 8, FastWAM and DiffWAM instead share the
same task-trained FastWAM checkpoint as their starting
point, with the additional Diff pathway introduced for Dif-
fWAM. Both receive one additional epoch of training on
clean demonstrations under matched conditions and are eval-
uated in the clean setting. The reported success rates are
88.47% and 93.13%, respectively. This comparison controls
for the additional training budget and is separate from both
the clean-to-random evaluation and the full-data RoboTwin
result. No mechanistic claim about overfitting is made from
the FastW AM score alone.

B.1 Comparison Methods
We compare DiffWAM with representative vision lan-
guage action (VLA) and world action model (WAM) ap-
proaches. VLA methods directly map visual-language obser-
vations to actions, while W AM methods additionally model
action-conditioned dynamics.
VLAs
OpenVLA [5] and OpenVLA-O [6] are an open VLA base-
line and its optimized variant; MemoryVLA [56] augments
VLA control with memory. π0 [7] and π0.5 [8] are flow-
based VLA models, with the latter targeting open-world
generalization. GR00T-N1 [9] is a generalist humanoid foun-
dation model, X-VLA [59] targets cross-embodiment trans-
fer, ABot-M0 [ 60] learns an action manifold, and Qwen-
VLA [61] unifies VLA modeling across tasks and embodi-
ments.
W AMs
Motus [13] models latent actions in a unified world model;
LingBot-V A [14] uses causal world modeling; and Fast-
W AM [15] is the W AM baseline used for RoboTwin 2.0 ini-
tialization. ImageW AM [57], X-W AM [44], and WLA [58]
respectively explore image editing based WAM, 4D world
action modeling, and joint world language action modeling.

B.4 Real-world Results
Our real-world study evaluates DiffW AM-S on three ma-
nipulation tasks: Place Pens, Store Block, and Stack Cups.
We use approximately 1.1k demonstration trajectories in to-
tal and train for 63k optimization steps. Each method is eval-

the effectiveness of joint state and visual difference mod-
eling in this real-world setting. Broader evaluation across
objects, environments, and embodiments remains necessary
to establish generality.

C Limitations and Future Work
C.1 Limitations
Several limitations remain. First, the independent M-
layer Diff Expert pathway introduces additional computation
and memory during training. Freezing the LDB in DiffW AM-
S does not eliminate this cost, since diff predictions are still
computed and their gradients propagate into the shared rep-
resentation. Both the World and Diff Experts are removed at
inference, so these visual prediction pathways add no online
action-generation overhead. Second, the difference target
captures inter-frame latent variation without explicitly dis-
tinguishing changes caused by robot actions from camera
motion, illumination changes, or movement of unrelated ob-
jects. This ambiguity may be more significant in dynamic,
unstructured environments. Third, the clean-to-random result
establishes a benefit under the evaluated RoboTwin pertur-
bations, while absolute Random success remains low and
generalization to other shifts is not established. The experi-
ments cover a limited range of data scales and embodiments,
and DiffW AM is not pretrained on a large-scale robot dataset.
Broader evaluation is needed to establish how the benefits of
DiffW AM-S and DiffW AM-L generalize across training-data
regimes and deployment settings.
C.2 Future Work
Future work will extend DiffWAM in three correspond-
ing directions. First, more efficient Diff Expert designs, such
as lightweight prediction heads or selective temporal token
processing, could reduce the additional training cost and
enable scaling to larger backbones, longer horizons, and
richer multi-view observations. Second, we will investigate
action-conditioned, motion-aware, and object-centric con-
straints to disentangle action-controllable changes from nui-
sance dynamics; multi-scale and multi-horizon diff predic-
tion may further capture both fine-grained contact dynamics
and longer-term task progress. Third, large-scale pretraining
on diverse robot interaction data is a promising direction
for learning more transferable visual and action priors be-
fore downstream adaptation. Evaluating this setting across
different embodiments, camera configurations, objects, and
long-horizon real-world tasks will be important for establish-
ing the generality of diff prediction.
